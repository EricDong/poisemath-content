*This month’s research brief includes strong evidence that **when and how** learning happens matters more than how it feels. New studies show that spacing may work partly by increasing social–cognitive alignment with the teacher, that students systematically misjudge effective strategies like interleaving, and that core concepts and schemas actively change with use rather than remaining stable. *


*We also see clear evidence that delayed effects are real not illusory, from Montessori preschool gains emerging at kindergarten to practice with feedback outperforming lectures for memory and transfer. Across reading, instruction, classroom practice, and AI, a consistent finding is that durable learning depends on knowledge structure, task sequencing, and instructional design, not motivation, intuition, or surface engagement.*

### Memory, Learning, and Knowledge


[Is the spacing effect partly a social effect in disguise? ](https://substack.com/redirect/0ec223fd-cceb-4a94-8cd2-c54b2b988e78?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This laboratory study compared spaced and massed instruction for undergraduate students learning psychology concepts via instructional videos. Both groups received identical content, but the spaced group encountered repetitions separated by unrelated material, whereas the massed group received consecutive repetitions. Learning outcomes favoured the spaced group, with a statistically significant and large reported effect. Crucially, the spaced condition also showed greater teacher student interpersonal brain synchronisation, measured using EEG hyperscanning, particularly in frontal alpha activity.


What makes this study distinctive is that the spacing advantage was statistically linked to students’ perceptions of teaching quality and interaction. Higher interpersonal brain synchrony predicted better learning outcomes, but this relationship was mediated by positive evaluations of teaching. In plain terms, spaced instruction appeared to work better because students felt more cognitively and socially connected to the teacher, even in a video based, one way instructional context.


[The "feeling" of learning is highly deceiving: Students use "fluency" (how easy something feels) as a proxy for success, but fluency is often a sign that no long term memory is being built.](https://substack.com/redirect/e5baf0d7-4de2-4afd-9341-84e44249a7a0?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study investigates the “misinterpreted effort hypothesis”—the idea that students reject effective strategies like interleaving (mixing topics) because the struggle makes them feel like they are failing. Researchers tested whether “reframing” this struggle could change behaviour. They told one group that effort is a necessary “investment” for growth, while another group viewed it as a “requirement” of the task. The study found that when effort was framed as an investment, students correctly identified that the harder strategy was more effective for their learning.


For educators, this reveals a frustrating “knowing doing” gap. Even when students are convinced that a difficult strategy is better for their brains, they still tend to choose the easier path of “blocking” (focusing on one topic at a time). It suggests that simply telling students “struggle is good” might change their beliefs, but it won’t automatically change their study habits. Teachers may need to mandate effective strategies rather than leaving them to student choice, as the allure of “easy” learning is a powerful barrier to overcome.


[Do our concepts quietly change as we use them? Repeated analogical comparisons subtly reshape the relational schemas we rely on, meaning our concepts drift over time rather than staying fixed.](https://substack.com/redirect/fb3a9bbb-0b81-4662-af95-89bcc6cbd1c1?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This paper tests a largely unexamined assumption in analogy research: that abstract schemas formed from analogical reasoning remain stable once learned. Across three tightly controlled experiments, participants repeatedly compared sentence pairs sharing the same abstract relation (such as RELEASE or AVOID) across very different surface contexts. The key finding is simple but important: when the same relational schema is activated again and again across disparate domains, people later judge even near-identical instances of that schema as less similar than before. The authors interpret this as evidence that the schema itself has changed or drifted.


Why this matters is that schemas are supposed to be the cognitive workhorses of transfer. They help us recognise deep structure and apply knowledge flexibly. If schemas drift with use, then transfer is not just about applying stable abstractions, but about abstractions themselves being plastic and context sensitive. This reframes analogical reasoning as a dynamic process that gradually reshapes conceptual structure, not merely one that retrieves it.


We think of abstract schemas as stable scaffolds, but new evidence suggests they may drift with repeated use. In other words, the more you stretch a relational concept to fit diverse situations, the less it may fit the original one.


[New research on measuring cognitive load suggests that asking students "was that hard?" at the end of class gives you biased, unreliable data](https://substack.com/redirect/5e62d709-2453-4d4c-a39f-f9b7f8960ac4?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs).


Researchers tracked 360 secondary students as they worked through complex chemistry worked examples. Unlike typical studies that ask for feedback only after a task is finished, this study interrupted students at seven distinct points to measure their invested mental effort, perceived difficulty, interest and self assessed understanding. The findings were clear: these variables are volatile, not stable. Mental effort and difficulty spiked during complex conceptual sections and dropped during introductory or closing sections, while interest and self assessed understanding did the exact inverse. Crucially, the study showed that a single "average" score calculated at the end hides these critical peaks and troughs, failing to represent the true cognitive demand placed on the learner.


Cognitive load is not a single, stable thing that students carry through a lesson. It rises, falls, peaks, and interacts with motivation in ways that matter. A task that looks manageable on average may contain moments of overload that derail learning. Equally, interest can drop sharply even while students persist, which has implications for how long we push through demanding material without instructional support.


[Creativity depends on what is in memory, not how long you think. This study shows that most correct solutions in a classic creativity task are produced accurately and intuitively under time pressure, with deliberation often adding little beyond what fast associative thinking already delivers](https://substack.com/redirect/3cfa8028-c1bc-4632-aab8-8b6e433022a8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This paper tested a core assumption in creativity research: that convergent thinking requires slow, controlled reasoning. Using the Compound Remote Associates task, participants first gave an intuitive answer under heavy time pressure and cognitive load, then gave a final answer with unlimited time. Across two preregistered experiments, roughly two thirds of all correct final answers were already correct in the initial intuitive stage. In other words, people usually did not need to think harder to think better. Deliberation helped somewhat, but it was not the main driver of success.


The authors then asked why some intuitions are right. Using semantic modelling and network analyses, they showed that intuitive success depends on the structure of knowledge. Problems with a smaller semantic search space were more likely to be solved intuitively, and individuals with denser, more interconnected semantic memory networks were far better intuitive problem solvers. Creativity here looks less like effortful strategy and more like rapid access to well organised knowledge.


For educators, the message is blunt. Fluent intuition is not guesswork. It is what knowledge rich minds do under pressure. If pupils struggle to solve problems quickly, the issue is unlikely to be that they are not thinking hard enough. It is far more likely that they lack the semantic structure that allows ideas to connect automatically. Building knowledge networks matters more than teaching generic creativity strategies.

## Instruction and Practice


[Team teaching or solo teaching? Does having two teachers really help pupils learn more?](https://substack.com/redirect/061375aa-ff04-4de5-9bd1-af5c2d0557fa?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study tested whether team teaching by two general primary teachers actually improves pupil achievement compared with traditional solo teaching. Using a crossover design, 267 pupils aged 10–12 experienced both conditions across natural science lessons. The analysis used multilevel item response modelling, allowing the authors to look at performance at the level of individual test items rather than blunt test totals. Pupils were more likely to answer questions correctly after team taught lessons, but the effect was modest: an odds ratio of 1.31 is real, but not transformative.


Where the study becomes more interesting is in its attention to context. The benefit of team teaching shrank as the student teacher ratio increased. In other words, combining classes and adding another teacher did not magically compensate for larger group sizes. The advantage seems to come partly from increased teacher availability, not from collaboration alone.


[Children offered Montessori preschool showed stronger literacy and cognitive outcomes at kindergarten, not immediately after preschool.  ](https://substack.com/redirect/7639d9f3-5632-4d35-b1ee-c79de5e28f68?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study followed 588 children who entered competitive lotteries for 24 public Montessori schools across the United States, beginning at age three and tracking outcomes through the end of kindergarten. Children who were offered a Montessori seat were compared with children who were not, using intention-to-treat (ITT) and complier average causal effect (CACE) models. Roughly half of those offered Montessori remained in Montessori through kindergarten, reflecting real-world attrition rather than idealised implementation.


The central and striking finding is temporal: Montessori showed no notable advantages at the end of PK3 or PK4, but clear advantages emerged by the end of kindergarten. Montessori students outperformed controls in early reading, executive function, short-term memory, and theory of mind, with ITT effect sizes above 0.20 SD; large by field-experiment standards. This pattern directly contradicts the typical preschool literature, where early gains fade by kindergarten.


[Practice beats lecture for memory, even with minimal feedback.](https://substack.com/redirect/e20a1d63-96cb-4b6d-9491-efd25ef883c9?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This paper tested whether practice with feedback can replace upfront instruction rather than merely follow it. In two preregistered experiments with nearly 600 adults, the authors compared traditional video lectures with practice centred learning using either simple correct answer feedback or explanatory feedback. Study 1 used multiple choice questions. Study 2 used open ended questions with personalised feedback generated by GPT-4o. Outcomes included memory, generalisation, metacognitive calibration, motivation, and learning efficiency.


The results were clear and nuanced. Practice with feedback reliably outperformed lecture for memory, even when feedback only provided the correct answer. For generalisation, however, explanation mattered. Learners only matched or exceeded lecture when feedback explained underlying principles, and even then only if they had sufficient prior knowledge. Practice with feedback also substantially improved metacognitive calibration, reducing overconfidence, and did so more efficiently than lecture, delivering more learning per minute.


[Does switching tasks actually make you better at switching next time?](https://substack.com/redirect/fc967ec7-5a78-4bde-859b-91fcd50bab91?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


Across four preregistered experiments, participants completed tasks arranged in short two-task “chunks”, separated by self-paced breaks. Crucially, this design reduced simple carry-over effects from repeating the same task and allowed the authors to isolate something more abstract: whether *the act of switching itself* changes how ready people are to switch again. The results were consistent and robust. After a chunk that involved a task switch, participants showed lower switch costs in reaction time and were more likely to choose to switch tasks voluntarily on the next chunk. This pattern held even when hand use, screen location, and task identity were controlled, and partially transferred to entirely different task pairs.

### Reading and Language Development


[Buying a knowledge rich curriculum does not solve comprehension if teachers are not taught how to think about meaning. Even after years of faithful use of high quality, knowledge rich reading curricula, most classroom comprehension instruction remains shallow, with only a minority of lessons supporting genuinely deep understanding of text.](https://substack.com/redirect/266e6e4c-1729-4086-820e-b3830f3e0c76?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study examined how comprehension focused high quality instructional materials were being implemented in four large US districts that had adopted leading knowledge rich curricula such as CKLA, Wit and Wisdom, and EL Education at least five years earlier. Using detailed classroom observations, surveys, and interviews, the researchers found that teachers were using these materials regularly, devoting substantial time to reading, and actively engaging pupils. On the surface, implementation looked strong. However, closer analysis showed that roughly two thirds of lessons only supported surface level comprehension, focused on completing tasks or meeting isolated standards rather than building a coherent mental model of the text.


What matters for teachers is the uncomfortable implication that curriculum adoption, even when done well and sustained over time, does not automatically translate into deep learning. Simply asking literal or even inferential questions is not enough if those questions are not oriented towards the meaning of the whole text. Robust comprehension required teachers to consistently frame lessons around big ideas, model meaning making, activate relevant prior knowledge, and press pupils to integrate details into a larger interpretation. The difference was not the curriculum but how teachers used it.


[Picture books help sometimes, not always, and rarely on their own. Across 20 studies, picture book based interventions show a moderate positive association with literacy related outcomes, but the evidence is highly uneven and methodologically fragile. ](https://substack.com/redirect/6b770e78-96f8-4445-b9bd-7510378eaa66?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This meta analysis pooled data from 20 studies involving just over 2,100 learners to estimate the impact of picture books on literacy, language, and vocabulary outcomes. The overall effect size was moderate to large on paper, but this headline figure masks substantial variation between studies, with high heterogeneity and clear signs of publication bias. Extremely large effects were reported for fifth grade pupils and for scientific literacy outcomes, but these came from very small subsamples, making them statistically unstable and likely inflated.


For teachers, the study does not show that picture books are a magic bullet for literacy. What it suggests instead is that picture books can support learning when they are well chosen, tightly aligned to instructional goals, and embedded within coherent teaching sequences. The benefits likely come from how pictures and text are integrated and used instructionally, not from the mere presence of illustrations.


[Study of 364 students finds that morphological awareness is a top predictor of reading efficiency in Grades 3–5.](https://substack.com/redirect/1a56a639-16b5-4aba-bf4f-618d3f53c1d4?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


Researchers examined how vocabulary (both shallow breadth and deep semantic knowledge), reading fluency, and metalinguistic awareness relate to reading efficiency in 364 Spanish-speaking children in grades 3 to 5, using a structural equation model. They showed that metalinguistic awareness (phonological and morphological skills) had the largest direct effect on reading efficiency, while vocabulary had a smaller direct effect, and fluency influenced efficiency largely through vocabulary. Vocabulary and fluency were also directly related, suggesting that quicker, more accurate decoding supports lexical knowledge, which in turn supports efficient reading.


This reinforces the idea that teaching phonological and morphological awareness is likely to yield bigger gains in children’s reading efficiency than focusing on vocabulary alone at these ages and in transparent orthographies like Spanish. It also highlights that vocabulary still plays a role, particularly as readers develop and use fluent decoding skills to build lexical knowledge.


[Does slowing down when reading actually mean students are thinking more deeply? ](https://substack.com/redirect/d84b629a-5ff1-4762-9dc9-5082adbbc298?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


The study investigated how readers detect inconsistencies in short expository texts and whether different kinds of reading behaviour signal genuine comprehension monitoring. Ninety-six university students read consistent and inconsistent texts while their eye movements were tracked. The key finding was that disruptions during first reading were mostly *not* linked to successful detection of inconsistencies. Instead, later reanalysis such as rereading or revisiting the specific word or sentence that caused the inconsistency strongly predicted whether readers correctly identified it


 In plain terms, struggling through a text more slowly did not help. What helped was noticing something was off and deliberately going back to recheck that exact piece of information.


For teachers, this undermines the idea that slower reading or increased effort automatically equals better comprehension. Students who reread everything indiscriminately actually performed worse, especially when texts were consistent. Effective comprehension monitoring looked targeted and purposeful. This supports teaching students to pause, question, and selectively reread key sentences rather than encouraging blanket rereading or generic “read more carefully” advice.

### Classrooms, Behaviour, and Attention


[Is humour a good strategy in the classroom? Are funny teachers more effective than serious ones?](https://substack.com/redirect/10321371-7d66-44d4-93b6-54918163a09b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This paper reports two controlled experiments using carefully matched video lessons that differed only in the type of humour used by the teacher: course-related, course-unrelated, self-disparaging, aggressive, or none. Study 1 asked experienced teachers to judge teaching quality; Study 2 asked secondary students to report perceptions, motivation, and emotions. The headline result is clear and uncomfortable for “just be engaging” narratives: only course-related humour reliably improved teacher-student relationships, perceived interestingness, intrinsic motivation, enjoyment, and reduced negative emotions. Aggressive humour consistently damaged relationships and increased anger and anxiety. Other affiliative forms of humour were inconsistent and often ineffective.


Bottom line: Only humour that is tightly tied to lesson content improves teaching quality, while other humour types are neutral at best and actively harmful at worst.


[Student perceptions of teaching and classroom management link to both confidence and achievement in primary maths.](https://substack.com/redirect/1a081ecc-8831-4819-8f32-36ebf1f73e1d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This large-scale analysis finds that fourth-grade students’ perceptions of classroom management and instructional clarity are meaningfully associated with both their mathematics achievement and their confidence in mathematics — with different patterns at the classroom and individual levels.


Key findings show that, at the classroom level, better aggregated classroom management relates strongly to higher average mathematics achievement. At the individual level, students who perceive stronger classroom management tend to *also* perform better, while instructional clarity relates more to students’ confidence in maths than direct achievement. The study also explores how these associations may differ across student backgrounds like socioeconomic status and immigration.


[Does five minutes of movement really sharpen maths thinking or is that wishful thinking? A short burst of physical exercise before maths does not boost general thinking skills, but it does modestly improve maths specific inhibitory control when it is immediately followed by maths instruction.](https://substack.com/redirect/25c85928-bee7-4c3a-b039-011ad2d3dd75?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study tested whether short bouts of physical exercise before a maths lesson improve executive function in ways that matter for learning. Four Grade 5 classes were assigned to one of four conditions: passive sedentary activity, active sedentary activity, five minutes of exercise, or ten minutes of exercise. Pupils then completed maths lessons, with executive function measured using both a general task and a maths specific task. The key finding was narrow but clear: pupils who exercised performed better on a maths specific inhibitory control task after instruction, but showed no consistent benefits on a general executive function task.


What matters for teachers is the precision of the effect. This was not a general cognitive boost or a magic brain upgrade. The benefit only appeared when the exercise was brief, immediately before maths, and measured using a maths relevant task. Longer exercise was no better than shorter exercise, and there was no evidence that exercise improved broad attention or self control across tasks. If teachers use movement breaks, they should see them as a way to prime readiness for a specific lesson, not as a general learning enhancer.


[Do great teachers teach well because of what they know or because of who they are?](https://substack.com/redirect/0a8fba60-b54e-4a6b-8f8c-d53cd7058162?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This meta-analysis synthesised 2,122 correlations from 197 studies, examining how a wide range of teacher characteristics relate to instructional quality, defined through the now-standard three dimensions: cognitive activation, learning support, and classroom management. The authors examined professional knowledge, beliefs, motivational orientations, and occupational self-regulation, both separately and simultaneously using meta-analytic structural equation modelling.


The headline finding is sobering: most relationships are small, highly heterogeneous, and sensitive to how variables are measured. Professional knowledge, often assumed to be the main driver of instructional quality, showed weaker and less consistent relationships than expected, especially for learning support. By contrast, motivational orientations and occupational self-regulation showed more consistent links across all three dimensions, though still modest in size.


This study challenges the comforting idea that improving teacher knowledge alone will reliably improve teaching quality. Knowledge matters, but it does not translate cleanly or automatically into classroom practice. Instructional quality appears to depend as much on teachers’ motivational commitment and capacity to regulate effort and stress as on what they know. In practical terms, this suggests that teacher development focusing only on “knowing more” is unlikely to move the needle unless teachers are also supported to sustain effort, manage demands, and stay engaged.

### SEN, Individual Differences and Inclusion


[Among novice special education teachers, teaching related self efficacy is strongly linked to better mental health, while subject related and teaching related motivation show no meaningful relationship with well being or symptom severity. ](https://substack.com/redirect/48c3c129-3372-4b2e-a838-b57274a0969f?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study surveyed 695 first year special education teachers in southern Germany to examine whether different motivational orientations are linked to mental health. Using structural equation modelling, the authors tested subject related motivation, teaching related motivation, and teaching related self efficacy against two outcomes: well being and psychological symptom severity. The central finding is blunt: only teaching related self efficacy mattered. Teachers who felt more capable of handling professional challenges reported substantially higher well being and fewer mental health symptoms. Enjoying teaching or liking the subject did not protect mental health once self efficacy was accounted for.


Bottom line: Feeling enthusiastic about teaching, or passionate about a subject, is not enough to buffer early career stress in special education. What matters is confidence in managing complexity: behaviour, emotional labour, coordination with others, and problem solving under pressure. If training programmes focus heavily on ideals, values, or intrinsic motivation without systematically building real competence and mastery, they are likely missing the lever that actually supports teacher mental health.


### AI, Technology, and Cognitive Tools


[AI generated personalised feedback achieved 90 to 98% accuracy across multiple response categories with zero hallucinations, demonstrating a viable pathway for scaling explanatory feedback.](https://substack.com/redirect/e20a1d63-96cb-4b6d-9491-efd25ef883c9?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This study examined whether students can learn effectively from practice and feedback alone, without first receiving traditional instruction. Across two experiments with 597 participants learning statistics concepts, the researchers compared a video lecture against practice with either simple correct answer feedback or elaborated explanatory feedback. For memory outcomes, both forms of practice outperformed lecture by roughly half a standard deviation and achieved this in approximately 24% less time. For generalisation to novel problems, however, only explanatory feedback matched the lecture's effectiveness, and crucially, this benefit emerged only for learners with adequate prior knowledge or mathematical confidence.


Crucially, the AI feedback was paired with static visual explanations identical to those in the human authored condition, meaning the personalisation was in the textual diagnosis and correction rather than in the conceptual instruction itself. This hybrid approach, combining human designed scaffolds with AI generated adaptive text, may represent a more realistic near term model for educational AI than fully autonomous tutoring. The study also leaves open whether AI feedback would maintain accuracy and pedagogical quality across more complex domains, longer learning sequences, or topics where correct answers are less determinate than statistical interpretation.


Educators considering similar implementations should note that the high accuracy depended on substantial prompt engineering, including worked examples of correct and incorrect responses drawn from pilot data, suggesting that effective AI feedback requires significant upfront investment in specifying the knowledge to be assessed.


[Can AI feedback really build teachers’ confidence as well as a human mentor can?](https://substack.com/redirect/3a463cd0-4501-4638-b540-071e8887296c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)


This quasi-experimental study compared AI-generated feedback with teacher feedback during simulated microteaching sessions for pre-service maths teachers in China. Both groups improved significantly in teaching self-efficacy across student engagement, instructional strategies, and classroom management. In other words, structured feedback of either kind helped novices feel more capable after teaching practice. However, teacher feedback led to larger gains in student engagement efficacy and was consistently rated as more credible and more useful than AI feedback.
