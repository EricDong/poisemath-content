# The Memory Ceiling: What AI Teaches Us About Human Learning

Subscribe here for more￼The Memory Ceiling: What AI Teaches Us About Human LearningHow the hard limits of memory drive learning and deeper understandingCARL HENDRICKSEP 13 ￼￼￼￼READ IN APP￼ When people talk about artificial intelligence, there’s often an assumption that these systems have infinite memory. With billions of parameters and terabytes of data, surely they just swallow the internet whole? But a new study shows this picture is not accurate. Large language models, it turns out, have hard limits to what they can “store.” Beyond that ceiling, they stop copying examples and begin to extract patterns.This finding matters not only for AI safety and policy, but also for education. It offers a surprisingly clear analogy to how human learning works: as I’ve argued previously, the limits of memory are not a weakness, but a gateway to deeper understanding through a kind of compression or distillation of information into transferable patterns.The finding that both artificial and biological systems benefit from forgetting specifics in favour of extracting patterns touches on fundamental questions that have occupied philosophers for millennia. At its core lies an ancient tension: is genuine knowledge about particulars or universals?Some 2,300 years ago, Aristotle distinguished between mere experience (empeiria), which arises from remembering many particulars, and true knowledge (epistēmē), which comes from grasping the universal principles that bind those particulars together. Plato, too, cast suspicion on memory as the ground of wisdom, arguing in the Meno and the Phaedrus that recollection and dialectic reach beyond the accumulation of facts toward enduring forms. The paradox that forgetting details may open the way to deeper understanding is not new: it is a very old problem, and one that AI research has now stumbled into afresh.When language models abandon memorising individual examples in favour of extracting statistical patterns, they echo this ancient Platonic insight: understanding emerges not from cataloguing every particular thing, but from grasping the universal relationships that connect them.A Ceiling on MemoryIn this study, the researchers approached the problem through information theory, formally separating memorisation into two components: unintended memorisation (the information a model retains about specific training examples) and generalisation (the knowledge it acquires about underlying patterns in the data). They asked: how much can a language model really memorise at the sample level? Their method was elegant in principle. If a model compresses a text more efficiently than a strong reference model, it must have "remembered" something specific about that text. Using this framework, they trained hundreds of transformer models of different sizes and tracked how many bits of information they could hold.The result was strikingly consistent. No matter how much data was fed in, models plateaued at around 3.6 bits per parameter. This is their storage ceiling for unintended memorisation. Once a model hits that point, it can't just hoard every example. It must shift from verbatim recall to more general strategies: summarising, abstracting, and pattern-finding.This threshold also explains the much-discussed phenomenon of double descent. In classical statistics, error rates form a neat U-shape:error rates form a neat U-shape: when the model is too simple, it misses the pattern; when it is too complicated, it memorises instead of learning. But with modern neural networks, there’s a second act. After clinging too tightly to the data and as models scale further, error rates fall again. The reason? Once models exceed their memorisation capacity, they can no longer rely on rote storage. They are forced to generalise, and performance improves.￼The “double descent” curve: why both students and machines can fail by being too simple or too literal, but succeed when they move from copying to pattern-finding.From Notebooks to ExamsIf this sounds abstract, think of it in classroom terms. A student with a tiny notebook can’t capture enough and misses key content. Their exam performance will suffer from what computer scientists call “underfitting,” or failing because the learner is too limited.Now give them a larger notebook. They diligently copy everything word-for-word. It looks impressive: full pages, detailed notes, every example recorded. But when the exam comes, they struggle. Unless the question is exactly the same as their notes, they can't adapt. They've memorised without understanding. This is overfitting.Finally, imagine giving them a mountain of material, far too much to copy. Faced with overload, they begin to summarise, condense, and seek the underlying principles. They write fewer words but capture more meaning. On the exam, they can handle new contexts because they've built patterns, not just memories. This is the second descent: abstraction through necessity.It must be said however that although this comparison between AI and human learning is illuminating, we must acknowledge its limits. Language models and human brains operate through fundamentally different mechanisms. The paper measures memorisation through compression rates and information theory, using arithmetic coding to estimate how efficiently a model can represent text. Human memory, by contrast, involves complex neural processes; encoding, consolidation, retrieval etc. that we're only beginning to understand. Working memory constraints, forgetting curves, and the spacing effect all operate through biological mechanisms that have no direct equivalent in current AI systems.Yet the functional parallels are striking enough to warrant attention. Both systems face capacity constraints that force a choice between storing specific instances and learning general rules. Both benefit when those constraints push toward pattern extraction rather than rote retention.Beyond Machines: The Nature of UnderstandingThis raises a deeper philosophical question about the nature of knowledge itself. What does it mean to "know" something? Is it to have the fact stored, or to be able to regenerate it when needed? The paper leans towards the latter: the real strength of large models is not that they parrot the internet, but that they can produce plausible new outputs from learned patterns.Claude Shannon's information theory, (which underlies the above paper), measures information content purely in terms of surprise or unexpectedness. But this technical notion of "information" differs fundamentally from what we usually mean by knowledge or understanding. Shannon information is syntax without semantics; it tells us about the statistical properties of symbols but nothing about what they mean.This creates a philosophical puzzle. If AI models are merely compressing statistical patterns in text, how do they generate seemingly meaningful outputs? The gap between statistical compression and semantic understanding remains one of the deepest problems in philosophy of mind. The research findings don't resolve this puzzle, but they do suggest that whatever understanding these systems possess emerges from pattern extraction rather than memorisation.Predictive processing offers one compelling bridge across this gap. In this framework, brains are fundamentally prediction machines that build hierarchical models of the world and update them when predictions fail. The "surprise" that Shannon information theory measures (ie the unexpectedness of events) becomes the very signal that drives learning. When a language model successfully predicts word sequences, it has effectively built a model of the linguistic world those words describe.Meaning emerges not from stored semantic representations, but from the model's capacity to anticipate what comes next in various contexts. Consider reading "The cat sat on the..." and automatically predicting "mat." This prediction draws on compressed knowledge about cats, furniture, and typical scenarios. The meaning isn't separate from these statistical regularities, it is the set of successful predictions the regularities enable. Memory limits become crucial here: a system that remembered every irrelevant detail would build worse predictive models. Forgetting forces the lossy compression that enables robust prediction, suggesting that understanding itself might emerge from our capacity to compress experience into anticipatory patterns rather than store exhaustive records.The same principle applies to human understanding. We don't remember whole books word-for-word, but we can discuss their themes, arguments, and meanings. Deep reading, as Wolf has argued, is about building patterns of understanding that allow us to interpret new texts, not just recall old ones. Knowledge becomes less about the accumulation of data than the capacity to weave it into transferable structures.This challenges educational approaches that mistake information transmission for understanding. When we flood students with content without ensuring they can extract underlying principles, we risk creating the educational equivalent of an overfitted model: impressive performance on familiar problems, but brittle failure when contexts shift.The Librarian's ParadoxRobert Musil anticipated this insight in his novel The Man without Qualities¹. General Stumm, seeking "the greatest idea" for Austrian cultural unity, visits the State Library hoping to read his way to enlightenment. Faced with 3.5 million books, which would take 10,000 years to read at one per day, he despairs until he meets the head librarian.The librarian's secret? He never reads any of the books. "Anyone who lets himself go and starts reading a book is lost as a librarian," he explains. "He's bound to lose perspective." Instead, he reads only catalogs and tables of contents, mastering the relationships between books rather than their contents.￼Robert Musil at his writing desk.Musil is arguing that the librarian who reads everything would drown in specifics and lose sight of the whole. But the one who reads nothing except organisational structures, who compresses millions of books into navigable patterns, becomes the most knowledgeable person in the building. Like AI models forced beyond their storage capacity, he achieves superior performance through principled forgetting.Musil's insight predates information theory by decades, yet captures a contentious argument made by AI advocates: in a world of infinite information, wisdom lies not in accumulation but in compression. The librarian's catalogs are human algorithms, reducing vast complexity to manageable patterns. His expertise emerges not from storing content but from understanding relationships, which is exactly what happens when AI models move from memorisation to generalisation.The Gift of ConstraintsThe lesson from this is counterintuitive but sort of reassuring I think. Memory has limits. For machines, it’s about 3.6 bits per parameter. For humans, it’s a finite working memory and a tendency to forget. But these limits aren’t flaws. They are what force us to move beyond storage towards structure.If forgetting is essential to learning, what does this say about human nature? Well for me, it suggests that our cognitive limitations (finite working memory, the tendency to forget, the inability to attend to everything simultaneously) aren't design flaws but design features. They force us to abstract, to generalise, to focus on what matters most. The limits of memory are not the end of learning. They are where learning truly begins.All this illuminates something profound about the nature of intelligence itself: understanding emerges not from comprehensive storage but from selective compression, not from perfect memory but from a kind of adaptive forgetting. Both biological and artificial minds achieve their greatest insights not by remembering everything, but by knowing what to forget. Limits are not the enemy of learning, they are its engine.1I can highly recommend Musil’s book although I must admit that I read it in a time before the Internet, AI and kids.
