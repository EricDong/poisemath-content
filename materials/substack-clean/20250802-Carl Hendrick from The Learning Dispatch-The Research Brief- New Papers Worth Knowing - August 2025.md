# The Research Brief: New Papers Worth Knowing - August 2025

*This month’s research roundup has a few odd findings. From the claim that students don’t need full prior knowledge activation to benefit from instruction, to the mixed evidence on retrieval in maths, some of these have me scratching my head. Other studies highlight simple, high-impact strategies like prompting predictions during worked examples or using AI for retrieval, that can improve outcomes with minimal disruption. Also included: new insights on error reflection, concept mapping, EAL timelines, and the real cognitive costs of multitasking in class and staff meetings. *

-

[New research challenges assumptions about prior knowledge activation: full coverage may not be necessary for learning gains.](https://substack.com/redirect/64fa6cf4-bc4a-4fb8-b890-8ce3acdf510d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

This is interesting in light of Rosenshine’s principle of activating prior knowledge. It seems you can overdo this. The general theory here seems to be; less pressure, same results.  This experimental study investigated whether students need to activate *all* relevant prior knowledge before explicit instruction, or if partial activation is sufficient. Using a vicarious failure design, students were shown incorrect or incomplete solution attempts covering either a high or low number of conceptual components of the targeted concept (mean absolute deviation). Surprisingly, students who studied solution sets with only partial conceptual coverage learned just as well as those who saw more complete coverage, suggesting that partial prior knowledge activation may be "good enough" to scaffold learning.

For teachers, this means that when preparing students for new content, especially in subjects like maths, it may be sufficient to activate *some* relevant prior knowledge rather than aiming for a fully comprehensive recap. Overloading students with too many conceptual components in advance may not offer additional benefits and could even strain working memory. Instead, offering targeted examples, analogies, or problem situations that connect to one or two key concepts may better prime students for instruction, especially if followed by deliberate, well-structured teaching.

[Instead of just watching examples, asking “What comes next?” before revealing the next step can significantly boost learning.](https://substack.com/redirect/5b90985f-c8f9-46e9-9b09-b724eddc1d70?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

This study offers decent evidence that prompting students to *predict the next step* in a worked example (before showing it) can meaningfully improve learning. Instead of simply watching or reading worked-out problems, learners were asked to retrieve and articulate each next step out loud before being shown the correct answer. This small but powerful tweak improved performance on both recall and problem-solving tests after a week, particularly for recalling procedural steps. Crucially, the success of this strategy depended on learners actually retrieving the correct steps, highlighting that retrieval quality, not just quantity, matters.

So when using worked examples in maths, science, or other procedural subjects, teachers might pause before each step and ask students, “What comes next?” Whether done aloud, in writing, or via think-pair-share, this encourages active engagement and taps into the well-documented “testing effect.” Importantly, this approach avoids common pitfalls of previous retrieval-based designs that made learners solve whole problems too soon and risked failure without feedback. Instead, this stepwise method builds confidence while strengthening memory and understanding.

[Well-structured concept maps with more interconnections reduce ](https://substack.com/redirect/b9d8b697-e743-4be0-bf33-657ef0280c20?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[extraneous](https://substack.com/redirect/b9d8b697-e743-4be0-bf33-657ef0280c20?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ cognitive load by helping learners organise information, rather than overwhelming them with complexity.](https://substack.com/redirect/b9d8b697-e743-4be0-bf33-657ef0280c20?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

This study challenges the common assumption that adding more elements to instructional materials such as interconnections in concept maps, automatically increases cognitive load. In a controlled experiment with postgraduate students, those who studied maps *with* more interconnections reported *lower* extraneous cognitive load than those with simpler maps. Crucially, this effect did not increase the more essential cognitive demands of the task (intrinsic load) nor reduce the mental effort dedicated to learning (germane load). The added connections acted not as distractions but as organising supports, helping students make sense of relationships within the material more efficiently.

[Shallow reflections = shallow learning. Students who connect current errors to past mistakes and future applications demonstrate the highest quality learning-from-errors processes.](https://substack.com/redirect/20a9de61-1d61-4d55-84f2-1c5b6edaec5e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

Kind of obvious this one but all evidence is helpful. This study explores how Year 8 students reflect on errors in maths (specifically factoring problems), using a two-dimensional framework based on frequency (how often they engage in error-related strategies) and depth (how thoughtful and transferable their reflections are). Five student types emerged ranging from ‘ineffective thinkers’ who show little engagement, to ‘insightful reflectors’ who deeply analyse their mistakes and link them to underlying concepts. Crucially, students who wrote more often *and* more deeply about their mistakes tended to have higher academic achievement, suggesting that how students think about their errors really matters, not just fixing them.

For teachers, the takeaway is clear: simply asking students to correct mistakes isn’t enough. Instead, prompting students to *reflect on why* they made an error and *how* to avoid it next time can be a powerful tool for deeper learning. This might include encouraging them to categorise errors, compare current mistakes to past ones, and articulate the conditions under which certain maths procedures apply.

[Spaced repetition shows consistent gains in maths learning, but not so impressive results for retrieval practice alone. ](https://substack.com/redirect/6bd992ae-1cb6-4046-b08f-f3602e19285c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

Bit of a weird one this. This study pulls together evidence from 27 studies on spacing and 7 on retrieval practice in mathematics education. It shows that spacing does have a reliable, if modest, benefit for maths learning (effect size g = 0.28). Interestingly, the effect is stronger when the material is taught in isolated sessions (g = 0.43) rather than integrated into full courses (g = 0.24), suggesting that when you're introducing new concepts or skills, spacing might be particularly useful for consolidation. This is broadly consistent with what we know from cognitive science but also highlights that the benefits in maths may not be as pronounced as in other domains like verbal learning.

However, the picture for retrieval practice is less clear. The meta-analysis looked at seven studies comparing testing to restudy and found only a small, non-robust benefit (g = 0.18, with a confidence interval crossing zero). This suggests that while retrieval practice is generally effective across many domains, its impact in mathematics may be more variable (surprising). Weird. Why is this? Maybe because retrieval practice has shown strong effects in domains that rely heavily on factual or semantic memory (e.g., languages), where retrieving isolated facts or explanations is more straightforward. In contrast, maths often requires stuff like executing multi-step procedures, solving problems with variable surface features etc. This makes simple recall (as used in many retrieval practice studies) a less natural or effective fit.

My sense is that we still don’t fully understand the interaction between retrieval and spacing in more complex domains like maths. Retrieval might need to be more structured (stepwise, scaffolded, or example-based for eg) to show its value here. Or perhaps it’s not retrieval per se, but the *type* of retrieval that matters. Either way, we need more work that looks beyond flashcards and factual quizzes and explores how retrieval can be designed for procedures, not just propositions.

*If anyone has a good explanation for this, I’d appreciate hearing from you in the comments. *

[Students who used ChatGPT for retrieval practice significantly outperformed those who did not in their final exams](https://substack.com/redirect/2e5a8669-4491-415a-8803-a33b18ce9795?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

The paper explores how ChatGPT was used to support retrieval practice by generating objective questions and offering instant feedback to students preparing for a high-stakes final exam in a Malaysian university course. By comparing students who used ChatGPT-assisted practice with those who did not, the study found that the former group achieved significantly higher exam scores. This result aligns with robust evidence supporting retrieval practice as a strategy for consolidating long-term memory. The novelty here lies in the integration of generative AI as a scalable feedback mechanism, particularly useful in large class settings where personalised feedback is often unfeasible.

This is evidence that LLMs could meaningfully support learning when embedded into evidence-based strategies like retrieval practice. However, the study also notes that AI should not replace human feedback entirely, especially when addressing complex misunderstandings or offering deeper conceptual support. Teachers might consider using ChatGPT to help students generate quiz-style questions and test themselves, while still reserving class time or office hours to tackle more nuanced learning needs.

[How long does it ](https://substack.com/redirect/aec14afe-f06b-401e-ac8e-0d39cb7d8fdc?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[really](https://substack.com/redirect/aec14afe-f06b-401e-ac8e-0d39cb7d8fdc?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ take to learn English in school? AERO’s latest report says 5–7 years, not months. Essential reading for educators of multilingual students.](https://substack.com/redirect/aec14afe-f06b-401e-ac8e-0d39cb7d8fdc?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

This comprehensive study by the excellent Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO) draws on a wide range of longitudinal data to estimate how long it takes students learning English as an additional language or dialect (EAL/D) to reach proficiency. The report finds that it takes, on average, 4–6 years for primary students and 6–8 years for secondary students to develop the academic English needed to access the curriculum. These timelines are consistent with international research but are often underestimated in practice. The authors stress that proficiency in conversational English often precedes academic English by several years, which can lead to false assumptions about a student’s readiness for complex academic tasks.

For classroom teachers, this means adjusting expectations and providing long-term, scaffolded language support well beyond the first few years of schooling. Standard assessment frameworks may fail to capture the true language development needs of EAL/D students, especially when they no longer receive specialist support. Teachers should therefore be wary of interpreting conversational fluency as full academic readiness. Ongoing formative assessment and explicit teaching of academic language structures are crucial, as is collaboration between mainstream and EAL/D specialists to support both language and content learning.

[Checking emails during video meetings doesn't just hurt your performance, it increases emotional, social, and motivational fatigue. ](https://substack.com/redirect/8ead2a50-0f2e-424f-9c02-470b051afb9d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

This two-part study comprising an experimental design and a longitudinal follow-up, examined the effects of multitasking during online meetings on cognitive fatigue and task performance. Participants who were asked to complete a secondary task while attending a videoconference experienced increased general, emotional, motivational, social, and (to a lesser extent) visual fatigue. Crucially, their actual performance on both note-taking and proofreading tasks declined significantly, while their perception of how well they performed did not improve—and in some cases worsened. This contradicts the popular belief that multitasking enhances productivity and shows instead that it introduces invisible costs.

For educators, this research carries strong implications for online teaching and staff meetings. Encouraging students (and staff) to “stay present” during videoconferences isn't just about courtesy, it affects their cognitive stamina and performance. If students are expected to multitask (e.g., “take notes while watching a video and contributing to chat”), they may appear productive but actually learn less and feel more exhausted. Similarly, during staff meetings or professional development sessions, multitasking (like answering emails) may diminish retention and decision-making. The takeaway: designing focused, interactive, single-purpose online experiences—without competing demands—supports both learning and wellbeing.

[Retrieval-based concept mapping can be as effective as traditional retrieval practice in supporting long-term retention and transfer of knowledge, making it a viable strategy for deep learning](https://substack.com/redirect/6ecaf360-0caa-4268-b248-ef9b8ec4edfa?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs).

This study compared three different learning strategies; rereading, retrieval practice through questions, and retrieval-based concept mapping, to see which best supports long-term learning and the ability to transfer knowledge to new situations. Although students who practised retrieval by answering questions initially recalled more than those who used concept mapping, both strategies led to significantly better performance on a transfer test a week later compared to rereading. Crucially, the concept mapping was structured as a retrieval task where participants had to build their map from memory in response to the same guiding questions used in the retrieval practice condition.

For teachers, this means that if concept mapping is framed as a form of retrieval (rather than a note-taking or summarisation activity), it can support meaningful, long-term learning and help students apply what they’ve learned in new contexts. This could be especially useful in subjects where understanding relationships and structures between ideas is key—like science, history, or geography. Concept mapping might also be a helpful alternative for students who benefit from more visual or spatial approaches to thinking, as long as the maps are generated from memory, not copied from notes.
