# Learning in the Age of Algorithmic Video

*Watch a teenager watch a video in 2026. Chances are it is playing at 1.75x speed, with subtitles on by default, on a phone propped against a textbook, while ChatGPT sits in another tab waiting to summarise the bits they didn’t catch. The video she is watching is, increasingly, not made by anyone in particular: it is delivered by an AI generated avatar and trained to look engaging within the first 1.2 seconds. This is now the dominant form of media that children are consuming. *

*And yet the research we have to make sense of this was conducted on a learner who has since been replaced. [Rich Mayer’s seminal work on multimedia theory](https://substack.com/redirect/3634994e-608e-44b7-891a-367d125f243c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) is some of the most carefully replicated research in educational psychology. What has changed however, are the conditions in which those primitives now operate. The principles still hold; but the boundary conditions under which they were established are no longer the conditions students are in.¹ *

*That leaves educators, parents and designers in an unusual position: relying on a research base whose validity has visibly decayed, and where the boundary conditions of learning have dramatically shifted on two axes; student behaviour + video format. Then there is the wildcard of AI, which is less a third axis than an accelerant on the first two: changing how students engage with video, and changing what video itself is. How should we design learning with video in mind and what should parents need to know?*

### Things have changed much more than we think

Last week, the [Wall Street Journal published an investigation ](https://substack.com/redirect/0cf252ae-1ea9-4227-861f-7fa5df2abe38?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)into YouTube in American classrooms which is shocking on many levels. One mother in Wichita noticed her seventh-grader had developed a surprisingly detailed knowledge of a video game she’d banned at home. When she checked his school Google account, she discovered he had watched more than 13,000 YouTube videos on his school Chromebook in three months, many of them violent, some sexually suggestive.

This is not an edge case. According to the Journal’s reporting, YouTube is sometimes half of all student web traffic on school devices.[ A survey cited by YouTube executives ](https://substack.com/redirect/54c6f6ea-6d26-4a4c-86c4-7d0fe5d3d6ff?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)found that 94% of teachers have used the platform in their roles. More than 88% of US public schools have issued 1:1 devices; Chromebooks (primed for YouTube by the way) hold around 60% of the K-12 mobile device market.

What none of these figures captures is what is actually happening cognitively while the watching is going on. To answer that, two shifts have to be in view: 1.) how students now watch, and 2.) how the medium itself has changed. I’ve been researching this for months now, trying to figure out how to apply the science of learning to a media ecology that did not exist when most of that science was done. What follows is a long post, an attempt to set out what we still know, what we don’t, and what educators, parents and designers should do in the meantime.

### What we now know about how students watch video

On the student side, video learning is now embedded in a thicket of habits that simply were not part of the laboratory paradigm from 30 years ago and which will seem alien to anyone over a certain age. Speed-watching at 1.5x or 2x is the default rather than the exception. Captions are on by default, even for native speakers, often with one word highlighted in yellow. The phone is usually the second screen, and often the first. [Cognitive offloading ](https://substack.com/redirect/3f105b05-33f2-4ab4-ab69-0273e13319c2?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)to AI assistants is a routine reflex, not an event. Students arrive at an instructional video with priors built from TikTok, YouTube, and the algorithmic feed, which means their tolerance threshold for what counts as cognitive load, or, conversely, as boredom.

On the medium side, the format itself has drifted from the narrated PowerPoint that multimedia studies treated as the canonical multimedia stimulus in 2001. The dominant grammar of educational video in 2026 is short-form, jump-cut, performer-on-camera, dense with kinetic typography, and increasingly AI-generated where convenient. Many videos are now delivered by avatars who do not exist. The decisions about face, pace, length and ornament are being made by content creators raised on a radically different set of YouTube production conventions, rather than instructional designers raised on cognitive load theory.

### **Need for speed**

Begin with speed. Speed-watching is the most empirically settled of the new behaviours, and the picture is more interesting than the simple “comprehension is preserved at 2x” headline would suggest. [Murphy and colleagues’ 2022 work at UCLA](https://substack.com/redirect/35b448d7-991f-4c2a-81ad-49925234b9ee?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) established the boundary: comprehension is preserved at 1.5x and 2x, with measurable degradation appearing at 2.5x and a steep drop beyond that. Their [survey of UCLA undergraduates](https://substack.com/redirect/ea15729a-6559-407e-9e80-29abe6111f5e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) put the proportion of speed-watchers at 85% several years ago, and that figure has now ossified into a generational default.

Three findings from the last eighteen months complicate the simple story. A [2026 ](https://substack.com/redirect/ad148c98-72a2-421f-8f2d-29e01cea026e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Frontiers](https://substack.com/redirect/ad148c98-72a2-421f-8f2d-29e01cea026e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ paper](https://substack.com/redirect/ad148c98-72a2-421f-8f2d-29e01cea026e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) shows that perceived clarity drops before objective comprehension does: students believe they are losing information at 1.5x even when they are not, and respond by speeding up further to compensate, which is precisely when comprehension begins to fail. [Ahn and colleagues, working in ](https://substack.com/redirect/e6458a1e-164d-4f77-a74e-7abbf59e1e9d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Applied Cognitive Psychology](https://substack.com/redirect/e6458a1e-164d-4f77-a74e-7abbf59e1e9d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ in 2025](https://substack.com/redirect/e6458a1e-164d-4f77-a74e-7abbf59e1e9d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), find that face-on-screen confers no benefit at higher playback speeds and may compete for attention when speech is already compressed. This is one of the few studies that addresses the boundary-condition question directly, and it is not encouraging: the instructor-presence effect that Mayer’s tradition documented may simply not survive the playback conditions in which video is now consumed.

A third paper, [from ](https://substack.com/redirect/82609441-7046-49ba-8228-d9145b2dbf8c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Computers in Human Behavior](https://substack.com/redirect/82609441-7046-49ba-8228-d9145b2dbf8c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ in early 2025](https://substack.com/redirect/82609441-7046-49ba-8228-d9145b2dbf8c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), finds that even where comprehension is preserved, metacognitive monitoring degrades. Students do not know what they are missing. The implication is that speed-watching is fine for material the student already partly knows, and particularly poor for material at the edge of their schema, which is the material instructional video is most often used for.

### **Captions are now the default**

Then there is the caption layer. To my knowledge no published study has tested caption-on viewing during instructional video specifically, but the underlying behavioural data is unambiguous. [Stagetext’s UK survey](https://substack.com/redirect/21b2fd28-567a-493d-8be0-3772aa96e76e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) reports that 80% of 18-to-24-year-olds use subtitles “some or all of the time.” [Preply](https://substack.com/redirect/3b240942-7c8d-4584-8762-6124e98e9fad?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) puts Gen Z subtitle usage at 70%, against 38% for boomers. [Netflix has 80% of its members regularly using subtitles or captions](https://substack.com/redirect/1271f986-beb2-4c3a-9065-cf328bddc8e1?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs). The reasons go well beyond accessibility: muddled audio mixing in modern productions, accent comprehension, viewing in noisy environments, and the dual-channel reading-while-watching habit conditioned by TikTok, where captions are baked into the medium rather than offered as an option.

This intersects awkwardly with the redundancy principle, which warns against on-screen text duplicating speech under conditions of split attention. Internal reporting from the [University of Bristol’s Digital Education Office](https://substack.com/redirect/aac58224-0358-4b96-90e3-07e1bdbd8539?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) on the accuracy of automatically generated captions for online learning materials suggests something subtler than the principle would predict: students may now process captions as part of the audio channel rather than a competing visual one. The boundary condition for the redundancy effect appears to have shifted under generational habituation. This is one of the cleaner candidate areas for new empirical work, because it is no longer obvious that what we currently call the redundancy principle is testing what it was originally designed to test.

Worth noting that the redundancy principle was never one of Mayer’s strong findings. In his [2024 retrospective](https://substack.com/redirect/cfb56518-caa6-4d04-8000-9d2750046379?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), it sits at d = 0.10 across 12 tests, among the weakest in the catalogue. The principle is being asked to do work in this argument that it never did particularly strongly even in the laboratory conditions in which it was first established. That makes the captions-on shift more interesting, not less: a weak principle whose weak evidence base now needs re-testing under conditions in which the very channel architecture it assumes has changed.

### **The second screen**

The third behavioural drift is the second screen. Industry data has 95% of consumers holding their smartphones while watching television, and 81% of students multitasking while doing homework. The educational research base is more cautious but consistent: multitasking during instructional content depresses transfer (the ability to apply what was watched to new situations) more than it depresses factual recall. Students can still tell you what was said. They cannot do anything with it. The *[Information Systems Research](https://substack.com/redirect/fe4b8160-5649-4e4b-ad19-68236a734244?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ paper “From Smartphones to Smart Students”](https://substack.com/redirect/fe4b8160-5649-4e4b-ad19-68236a734244?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) finds the now-canonical pattern, namely that any concurrent device use during instruction reduces comprehension, with a larger effect on higher-order outcomes. A more uncomfortable finding from the qualitative literature on Gen Z multitasking is that students do not experience the second screen as multitasking at all. They experience it as the floor of cognitive engagement, with single-screen attention feeling under-stimulated. That is a critical observation, because design decisions that assume single-screen attention as the default are now designing for a learner who does not exist.

With what we know about cognitive architecture, it’s hard to see how meaningful learning can happen under these conditions. But the idea that we don’t learn from video, or indeed AI-generated video, is a myth. Allow me a slight personal digression…

### The rich history of educational video

Twenty years ago I watched Kenneth Clark stand in front of Chartres Cathedral for thirteen hours. *[Civilisation: A Personal View](https://substack.com/redirect/7302b8d3-1ef7-484c-9113-5205045e31e7?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ ](https://substack.com/redirect/7302b8d3-1ef7-484c-9113-5205045e31e7?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)had been broadcast a quarter-century before I encountered it on a BBC repeat, but the series completely fascinated me, and changed how I thought about Western art, history and the long sweep of European intellectual life in ways I am still drawing on.

[![Civilisation: A Personal View by Kenneth Clark (1969) - Parts 6 through 9](../substack/_assets/20260508-Carl%20Hendrick%20from%20The%20Learning%20Dispatch-Learning%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20Algorithmic%20Video/https%253A%252F%252Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%252Fpublic%252Fimages%252F887ed8ba-d2e1-478e-9901-c033ba3249b2_1280x720-a3ab848971.jpeg)](https://substack.com/redirect/5e643d61-742f-4937-9840-7e6377c91e7e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

A few years later, reading American literature for my undergrad degree, the thing that catalysed my understanding of how modern America was formed, more than almost any seminar, was [Ken Burns’s ](https://substack.com/redirect/85bf25b5-fb2b-46a0-b49d-12d38bad02a8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[The West](https://substack.com/redirect/85bf25b5-fb2b-46a0-b49d-12d38bad02a8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[. ](https://substack.com/redirect/85bf25b5-fb2b-46a0-b49d-12d38bad02a8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)Eight hours across nine episodes, voiceover narration over still photographs, primary sources read aloud, Peter Coyote as the unobtrusive through-line. I came back to it repeatedly during the degree and after, and it did more for my sense of the geographical, racial, and cultural substrate of the literature than almost any classroom encounter. Later I watched *The Civil War *and was utterly entranced by [Shelby Foote and his slow Mississippi drawl, ](https://substack.com/redirect/870de834-1f1f-420e-90d6-5f0a3d44415c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)which made the war feel less researched than personally remembered.

[![](../substack/_assets/20260508-Carl%20Hendrick%20from%20The%20Learning%20Dispatch-Learning%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20Algorithmic%20Video/qCnK_4nj5Gc-620c406e93.jpg)](https://substack.com/redirect/3102574b-93cd-4dd5-8668-d95a864fc921?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

These videos brought the past to life for me in ways inseparable from how they were made: long, slow, deliberate, by people whose authority over the material had been hard-won and earned across decades.

Clark was a cultural tourist guide. A mind moving through history and reporting back what it found to 1970s British living rooms. But a new incarnation of that cultural tourist guide has emerged, one which makes the additional claim of being educational and so is worth examining here. [Chloe vs. History](https://substack.com/redirect/8a1a4eb2-6190-45a5-80ff-405d9951efe7?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) is perhaps the most successful example: a warm, personable young woman who travels through history reacting to what she finds, with over 151,000 subscribers across TikTok and Instagram. But unlike Clark, she is entirely AI-generated and I suspect this is one reason it’s hard to learn anything from her.

[![](../substack/_assets/20260508-Carl%20Hendrick%20from%20The%20Learning%20Dispatch-Learning%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20Algorithmic%20Video/https%253A%252F%252Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%252Fpublic%252Fimages%252F53e88dc6-d1fe-448a-bcd1-8d83af8a1bb2_1898x842-f639525aa7.png)](https://substack.com/redirect/fb5218d7-bb7c-4258-8f96-bccaa84605f0?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

Learning has a lot to do with the brain trying to reconcile multiple elements, often competing ones. (This for me is why cognitive load theory is one of the most important frameworks for instructional design). So my hunch is that if the brain is trying to process a human face which it consciously knows is fake but which it instinctively feels is real, this creates a kind of perceptual incongruence that might not be good for learning.

I see this as a kind of [uncanny valley,](https://substack.com/redirect/2e88a993-c9fe-4aa3-b5c3-88e6a82a3bd6?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) but one with real pedagogical consequences in the sense that every bit of working memory the brain spends reconciling that incongruity is working memory not available for the actual content. If the avatar's face is doing background work to be parsed, the schema-building work the video is meant to support has fewer resources to draw on.

However, we do have examples of AI generated/assisted content which is able to bring the past to life in ways that are truly incredible and genuinely educative. The cleanest example is Peter Jackson's *They Shall Not Grow Old* (2018). Jackson used machine learning to interpolate frame rates, colourise, and stabilise a hundred-year archive of First World War footage from the Imperial War Museum. He hired forensic lip-readers to verify what the soldiers were actually saying so the audio reconstructions matched the historical record. Foley artists added period-correct sound. The AI was doing the technical restoration; the historical content was the soldiers themselves.

[![](../substack/_assets/20260508-Carl%20Hendrick%20from%20The%20Learning%20Dispatch-Learning%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20Algorithmic%20Video/19j-rEdGiEI-1f61638905.jpg)](https://substack.com/redirect/c7783d9e-29f5-4433-94b5-23952fb8e222?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

The film succeeded I think, for the same reason Burns's *The Civil War* succeeded: a careful intelligence at the centre of it, organising real material with patience. It was screened in cinemas, used in schools, and treated seriously by WWI historians. It made a generation that had grown up seeing WWI as a sepia abstraction encounter the actual soldiers, the same age as them, looking back at them.

## The AI layer: a mixed picture

Cognitive offloading to AI assistants is the most active growth area in education research, and the 2024 to 2026 record is mixed in a way that is actually clarifying. On the negative side, the [PNAS 2025 paper on generative AI without guardrails](https://substack.com/redirect/b205ee32-099a-4859-8be6-b136a5442074?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) harming learning in high-school maths is the strongest single piece of evidence that ungated AI use during practice degrades subsequent independent performance. A [2025 RCT in ](https://substack.com/redirect/e394db81-7ba9-4d79-bc34-9b25584e4133?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Social Sciences & Humanities Open](https://substack.com/redirect/e394db81-7ba9-4d79-bc34-9b25584e4133?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* finds matched effects in adult learners. The recurring pattern across that literature is that the same tool acts as enhancer or crutch depending on how it is used.

Among the positive findings, a [2025 quasi-experimental study in ](https://substack.com/redirect/e6012eda-bb71-454a-9da2-bc837f825ac0?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Forum for Linguistic Studies](https://substack.com/redirect/e6012eda-bb71-454a-9da2-bc837f825ac0?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* reports that students who delegated lower-order tasks (mechanics, formatting) to AI showed greater improvements in standardised critical-thinking assessments. A *[Scientific Reports](https://substack.com/redirect/bd3370d9-05a1-470f-9fe8-49624b73424b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ paper on preservice teachers](https://substack.com/redirect/bd3370d9-05a1-470f-9fe8-49624b73424b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) finds the same pattern: strategic offloading correlates with academic gain, passive offloading does not. The single empirical finding most worth carrying into the design conversation comes from a [Frontiers paper on differential effects of GPT-based tools](https://substack.com/redirect/e9b134a2-d3e5-43cd-95b5-ab98866462fa?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs): the form of the interaction matters more than the presence of AI itself, and the effect is moderated by prior ability. Low-performing students benefit most from Socratic-style chatbots that engage them in dialogue; high-performing students are harmed most by summary tools that do the cognitive work for them. Embedding "ask the AI" features in instructional video is therefore not a neutral act; the design of the prompt structure, and the cognitive level of the user, jointly determine whether the feature aids learning or substitutes for it.

The newer and more under-researched behaviour is the substitution of AI summaries for engagement with source material. [Survey data puts university student AI use at 86–92%](https://substack.com/redirect/5ab6e2ec-e8cb-4e16-8e6d-990be4a98a80?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) depending on geography and definition, with summarising readings the second most common use case after general help with assignments. For instructional video specifically, this matters because the behaviour to design around is no longer “students watch the video,” but rather “students watch the video, ask the AI to summarise it, and revise from the summary.”

I think this could be a fruitful approach, but whether it is net-positive or net-negative depending on prior knowledge has barely been studied. The note-taking literature, as the *[Learning and Instruction](https://substack.com/redirect/5cda0977-3f45-4b54-85d0-7960ec1cf051?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ 2026 paper “The mind, not the pen”](https://substack.com/redirect/5cda0977-3f45-4b54-85d0-7960ec1cf051?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) reaffirms, establishes the pre-AI baseline: deep note-taking instruction substantially improves comprehension because it forces generative processing during viewing. The post-AI commercial landscape ([Otter](https://substack.com/redirect/ccf113e2-59b7-4529-a277-2bea81d5187c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), [Coconote](https://substack.com/redirect/5d565a91-1b8d-46cf-9b0b-1272ca970ee8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), [NoteGPT](https://substack.com/redirect/0d812633-c8bb-4f38-8aa5-93570813b496?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), [Mindgrasp](https://substack.com/redirect/17bd283d-5b74-4788-9688-c7994927173d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), [Sembly](https://substack.com/redirect/0f15eaed-10c7-4ccc-be85-3a180775c462?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)) is now displacing that activity by producing AI-generated notes from lecture transcripts, (which is almost certainly not going to lead to learning by itself, although there is a world where those transcripts could be turned into learning materials). There is no published comparison of student-generated to AI-generated lecture notes on transfer outcomes (as far as I am aware). That is one of the most consequential gaps in the field.

## **What the medium has become**

The format-side drift is less well evidenced than the behavioural drift, with two exceptions: short-form video, and AI-generated instruction.

The short-form picture has matured fast. The [August 2025 medRxiv systematic review ](https://substack.com/redirect/72a4d4c6-651a-4341-a93b-ac49d854be0d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[The Impact of Short-Form Video Use on Cognitive and Mental Health Outcomes](https://substack.com/redirect/72a4d4c6-651a-4341-a93b-ac49d854be0d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*, the [2025 narrative review on short-form video and sustained attention](https://substack.com/redirect/2b6965f1-fb8b-4b6b-98ca-4b250cd43cc5?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), and the [2026 systematic review on short-video addiction in adolescents](https://substack.com/redirect/55f9c270-7695-4f05-85ea-97fef7fdaaff?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) converge on the same finding. Heavy short-form video use is associated with measurable decrements in sustained attention, working-memory efficiency and inhibitory control, with effect sizes large enough to persist after controlling for prior cognitive ability and family background.

The neural evidence has filled in. A [2025 Frontiers fNIRS study](https://substack.com/redirect/229671ac-7081-4cb7-9288-d8ce7bf03938?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) reports reduced prefrontal activation during cognitively demanding tasks among heavy short-form users. An [EEG study by Yan and colleagues (2024)](https://substack.com/redirect/62e958d0-d49b-435e-b131-b6e8f1a6189f?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) finds reduced midfrontal theta power, a marker of executive function, in heavy users. A [2025 longitudinal paper on screen-media activities and brain function](https://substack.com/redirect/63e4fe3d-7dae-49e7-b954-9ab11ce7f490?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) reports altered connectivity in prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, amygdala and striatum: the executive and reward circuits both. The standard caveat applies. Most of this evidence is correlational, the causal direction is partly contested (selection effects matter, since adolescents with worse attention may be drawn to short-form video rather than the other way round), and the experimental work has been small-N and acute. But the convergence across designs, populations and modalities is strong enough that prudent policy should not wait for the field to settle the causal question.

There is a parallel, narrower literature on TikTok as an educational tool. The [2025 systematic review ](https://substack.com/redirect/9a1d8170-eabf-48f1-9516-2b61d27d95e1?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[TikTok in higher education](https://substack.com/redirect/9a1d8170-eabf-48f1-9516-2b61d27d95e1?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* reports modest learning gains in certain contexts (medical education, language learning, physical education), but the studies are largely satisfaction surveys and self-reported engagement, with little controlled comparison against alternative formats. There is a small evidence base for short-form educational video as engagement-positive, almost none for it being learning-positive in any rigorous outcome sense. Engagement, as the field has long known, is a necessary but [not sufficient condition for learning.](https://substack.com/redirect/fd4977f7-bd12-497e-98b9-03aef62eda94?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

[![](../substack/_assets/20260508-Carl%20Hendrick%20from%20The%20Learning%20Dispatch-Learning%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20Algorithmic%20Video/https%253A%252F%252Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%252Fpublic%252Fimages%252F1d1fbfc0-6734-4053-b67f-f1df931be51f_1280x720-a274df522e.jpeg)](https://substack.com/redirect/13affb01-4f7b-4041-b4bf-69c38939edd3?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)

Still from *[A Classroom Without Walls: Teachers Take Their Lessons to TikTok](https://substack.com/redirect/15a8e5a8-821b-4408-a3ec-be17df246918?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* (Edutopia, 2021).

However, algorithmic delivery is the format shift the educational research literature has barely engaged with. The communication-and-media literature is more useful here. A [2025 piece on algorithmic recommendations in young people’s everyday lives](https://substack.com/redirect/ea0a1d9c-dfab-41f3-b442-57853605cc57?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) finds that adolescents experience the algorithm as an environment rather than as a tool, and report a reduced sense of choice over content selection. A [UCL / Kent / ASCL 2024 study on TikTok algorithmic exposure](https://substack.com/redirect/a88a7061-d599-4545-85f3-3f180e58c5da?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) reports that after five days of TikTok use the proportion of misogynistic content recommended to test accounts increased four-fold.

The implication for design is that sequencing assumptions baked into traditional curriculum (we will show this clip after that one) have no purchase when the medium is feed-driven. A video can succeed brilliantly as a TikTok and fail entirely as the third unit of a sequenced course, because the medium expects each piece to stand alone.

Then there is AI-generated instructional video, where the empirical base has grown rapidly. [Xu and colleagues in the 2025 ](https://substack.com/redirect/7baf0ce1-e69b-46dd-ac07-0c60c3dc515c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[British Journal of Educational Technology](https://substack.com/redirect/7baf0ce1-e69b-46dd-ac07-0c60c3dc515c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* find equivalent cognitive learning outcomes between AI-generated and human-instructor video, with a small advantage for AI on retention and no significant difference on transfer. A [2024 experimental study in ](https://substack.com/redirect/65fc7d56-4048-46fb-973c-d20395aac360?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Computers & Education](https://substack.com/redirect/65fc7d56-4048-46fb-973c-d20395aac360?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* reports the same headline finding. A [2025 Frontiers rapid review](https://substack.com/redirect/9a7c9847-aeaa-4e2e-904f-822a366261e7?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) notes that 72% of students in one experimental group did not recognise that their instructor was artificially generated. The conservative voice is the 2025 *Educational Psychology Review* piece “[Looking Beyond the Hype](https://substack.com/redirect/27d13833-4223-43ea-a2e8-4c632eea4c1a?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs),” which finds cognitive-outcome equivalence but lower social presence; student preference still tilts toward human instructors.

The implication is significant I think. The instructor-presence literature was built on the premise that the instructor is a human being whose social, emotional and pedagogical signals carry information. AI-generated instructors decouple presence from instructorhood: the face is real to the viewer but not real in any other sense. None of the canonical instructor-presence findings have been re-tested under this condition. The early evidence suggests cognitive-outcome equivalence; the open question is whether the social-emotional effects that originally made instructor presence “matter” survive at all when the instructor does not exist.

Mayer himself, in his[ 2024 retrospective on CTML, ](https://substack.com/redirect/cfb56518-caa6-4d04-8000-9d2750046379?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)names social, affective, motivational and metacognitive processes as the very dimensions his theory has not yet incorporated, and identifies them as the priority work ahead. The dimensions on which AI-generated instructors most pressure his theory are the dimensions Mayer himself, surveying four decades, concedes have not yet been done.

What about the video as phone view? The vertical 9:16 format has empirical traction in marketing research and almost none in education research. Industry forecasts put video at roughly 70% of mobile traffic and the dominant aspect ratio at 9:16, which suggests that horizontal-versus-vertical viewing is no longer a meaningful design choice but a constraint. There is no controlled comparison of horizontal-versus-vertical learning outcomes on equivalent content. This is the most obvious empirical gap in the field, and one where designers must use principled judgement until evidence appears.

A final format-side observation worth flagging is parasocial engagement. The communication literature on parasocial relationships dates to Horton and Wohl in 1956, and the 2024 to 2026 educational work is thin but suggestive. A [2025 experience-sampling study in ](https://substack.com/redirect/d8cbbbaf-063d-4f6f-8deb-bd8e995d8285?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Media Psychology](https://substack.com/redirect/d8cbbbaf-063d-4f6f-8deb-bd8e995d8285?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* reports that parasocial bonds with virtual influencers form quickly and behave similarly to those with human influencers along most engagement dimensions. For instructional video the implication is uncomfortable: the warmth and motivational signals previously attributed to instructor presence may now be functioning parasocially, with the on-screen creator standing in for an actual pedagogical relationship the student does not have. Face-on-screen may “work” in the sense Mayer tested, but for reasons that have nothing to do with pedagogy.

### **A methodological problem the field has not yet faced**

Before I move to design implications, there is an awkward fact about the literature I have just summarised that deserves to be on the table. The most important methods paper to appear on video learning in this window is [Gijsen and colleagues in ](https://substack.com/redirect/3a54f3d2-f5d4-4889-9f04-f0d847ffe59b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Learning and Instruction](https://substack.com/redirect/3a54f3d2-f5d4-4889-9f04-f0d847ffe59b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ (2024)](https://substack.com/redirect/3a54f3d2-f5d4-4889-9f04-f0d847ffe59b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), comparing concurrent think-aloud, retrospective think-aloud and cued-retrospective reporting (where the student narrates over a replay of their own eye-tracking trace) for studying processing during instructional video. The finding is uncomfortable but useful: most of what the existing literature claims about processes during viewing (what students attend to, when they integrate, how they segment) is inferred from concurrent or retrospective verbal reports, both of which carry well-understood pathologies. Concurrent think-aloud reactively changes the very processing it measures, and is especially noxious with time-locked stimuli. Retrospective think-aloud suffers omission and post-hoc confabulation. Cued-retrospective reporting threads the needle by anchoring recall to gaze, but is itself still retrospection rather than ground truth.

The implication for everything I have said matters in two ways. When the literature claims “students attend to X” or “learners integrate Y,” the warrant is verbal-report data with the limitations Gijsen documents. Outcome data is the floor; process data is the ceiling, and the ceiling has visible cracks. Second, the field has not, by and large, re-tested its process claims under modern viewing conditions (speed-watching, second screens, captions on, AI assistance), all of which would compound the existing methodological problems. The asymmetry I keep returning to in this post (more behaviour-side evidence, less format-side evidence) is partly a function of the methods available. Behaviour can be studied with surveys and log data. Process during video requires verbal reports, and what we have of those is suspect.

What this means in practice is that we know less than the literature suggests about what students actually do cognitively during instructional video. The gap between confident process claims and the verbal-report data underlying them is wider in 2026 than it was when Mayer’s principles were established.

## **What to do in the meantime**

Taken as a whole, I think the unit of analysis is no longer the video but rather the learning *episode.* Speed, captions, second screens and AI summarisers all modify the encoding conditions in ways that no scaling of CTML’s principles can fully anticipate. Design decisions made under the assumption of attentive single-screen viewing are designing for a learner who is statistically rare. Treating the video as a self-contained intervention, when in practice it is being consumed inside a noisy multi-screen, multi-tool environment, is the central category error in current educational video design. The video is one element in a wider learning episode, and the surrounding architecture (what comes before, what comes after, what the learner is asked to do during) is what determines whether anything is encoded at all.

The design lever with the most empirical support for protecting learning under these conditions is embedded retrieval and prequestioning. The prequestion literature is now better characterised than at any previous point. [Carpenter and Fraundorf’s 2025 multilevel meta-analysis](https://substack.com/redirect/58dfae50-da87-4622-a758-a21b793a75d6?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) establishes that prequestions reliably enhance learning of targeted content (g = .66) but do not generalise: they do not increase learning of incidental material in the same resource (g = .01). Feedback amplifies the effect substantially. [Krebs, Braschoß and Eitel (2024)](https://substack.com/redirect/58eb89fb-6b0a-41a4-95e4-a462a92ba75b?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), [summarised by The Learning Scientists](https://substack.com/redirect/9e1dc7d5-d907-4ea6-aed4-db78fe12ddff?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs), show that the benefit of explainer videos only materialises when prompt-questions are provided. [Wong and colleagues (2024)](https://substack.com/redirect/9841b184-c740-4b78-bd50-ade5bbe733fc?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) examines the timing of low-stakes embedded quizzes in online videos. [Huangfu and colleagues in the 2025 ](https://substack.com/redirect/42a80788-d671-480d-aab4-00ac1ac78579?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[British Journal of Educational Technology](https://substack.com/redirect/42a80788-d671-480d-aab4-00ac1ac78579?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* shows that low-prior-knowledge learners benefit most from prequestions with simple feedback and are harmed by elaborated feedback (a cognitive overload result). Embedded prequestions and retrieval prompts convert passive viewing into active engagement at the lowest design cost, and with the strongest empirical base. If I had to pick one design recommendation to defend against everything I have described in this post, that would be it.

The strongest empirical base is not rhetorical. In [Mayer's own 2024 catalogue of fifteen design principles,](https://substack.com/redirect/cfb56518-caa6-4d04-8000-9d2750046379?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) the generative activity principle sits at d = 0.71 across 44 experimental tests, the largest evidence base for any single principle in the entire CTML edifice. When I recommend retrieval and prequestioning as the design lever to defend against everything in this post, I am reaching for the most replicated finding the theory has produced.

The format drift creates conditions under which the coherence principle is being tested without anyone formally testing it, and (per Gijsen) the methods to test it properly are themselves suspect. The dominant grammar of the medium (fast cuts, kinetic typography, sound design, b-roll) is precisely what Mayer would call seductive detail. The closest direct evidence is a [2025 multi-level meta-analysis in ](https://substack.com/redirect/5708e342-cd39-4b9a-9a52-e1653906045c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Educational Psychology Review](https://substack.com/redirect/5708e342-cd39-4b9a-9a52-e1653906045c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)* drawing on 177 effect sizes across 50 studies, which finds a small but reliable negative effect of seductive details on learning, mediated primarily by extraneous cognitive load. There is currently no controlled comparison of TikTok-style cadence against narrated-PowerPoint cadence on transfer outcomes. The conservative position, until that comparison exists, is to treat coherence as the principle whose boundary conditions have most clearly shifted, and to make conservative coherence choices in any segment intended to teach novel material. Not because we have direct evidence the seductive-detail effect persists at 2026 cadences, but because the principle’s cognitive basis is well-understood and the burden of proof should now sit with maximalism, not with minimalism. Designers who load their video with kinetic typography and rapid cuts because “that is what students respond to” are taking a bet against a well-supported theoretical principle, and the bet is uncovered by the available evidence.

Another key point is that videos give a very strong sense of learning that is often an illusion. [Becky Allen’s recent essay on her Substack ](https://substack.com/redirect/33f57249-0fae-4761-ba66-5c1e81020204?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[Falsifiable](https://substack.com/redirect/33f57249-0fae-4761-ba66-5c1e81020204?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)*[ ](https://substack.com/redirect/33f57249-0fae-4761-ba66-5c1e81020204?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)extended [Deslauriers et al.’s fluency-illusion finding](https://substack.com/redirect/9d015626-073b-4e5a-b2a0-82bd0df2ed70?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs) to AI-generated educational video. The same extension applies to YouTuber explainers, with sharper teeth. Deslauriers and colleagues showed that students in polished, fluent passive lectures rated their perceived learning higher than students in challenging active learning, and scored significantly lower on the actual assessments. The correlation between perceived and actual learning was negative. The mechanism was specific: presentational fluency produces a feeling of understanding that decouples from, and at high doses can substitute for, the effortful encoding that produces understanding.

One more design heuristic worth naming. AI tools embedded in video learning environments should be configured for dialogue, not for delivery. The empirical pattern is consistent: students who use AI to think (Socratic prompts, follow-up questions, generative tasks) gain; students who use AI to receive (summaries, answers, abridgements) do not. The product decision to embed an “ask the AI” feature in an instructional video is not pedagogically neutral. The shape of the prompt matters more than whether the feature exists.

## Three questions before “I learned so much from this video”

Becky’s diagnostic offers a clean way to handle any specific claim that one has learned something from a YouTuber explainer or a short-form video. Three questions tell you whether the claim is mechanistic or merely felt.

-

What kind of knowledge is being claimed? Is it factual recall (”I now know the population of Mongolia”), procedural-motor (”I can now truss a chicken”), conceptual understanding (”I now understand quantum tunnelling”), or transferable expertise (”I can now solve a class of physics problems I could not before”)? The format has different empirical credibility at each of these levels. It is most defensible at procedural-motor, plausible at factual recall with repetition, weak at conceptual understanding, and entirely speculative at transferable expertise.

-

What is the cognitive mechanism that produced the claimed knowledge? Was there retrieval practice? Was there elaboration? Was there generation? Did the video pause for self-explanation? Was the content revisited? Was the learner tested? If the answer to all of these is no, the only mechanism on offer is passive encoding from a fluent presentation, and the literature predicts that mechanism produces the felt experience of understanding without durable schema acquisition.

-

What would falsify the claim? The IOED methodology suggests an immediate test. Asked to write a detailed explanation of the topic without rewatching the video, can the learner produce one? Asked a transfer question that requires applying the principle to a new case, can the learner answer? If not, the claim of having learned is the fluency illusion in person. The video produced an experience. It did not produce a competence.

## **What the format is genuinely good for**

A fair accounting requires saying what these formats do well, because the polemical “screens are ruining everything” reading is no more empirically grounded than the [MrBeast reading ](https://substack.com/redirect/88b981f0-d5ad-47f6-80d6-e6e3419ce6be?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)(that students could learn more in 5 hours of YouTube than 8 hours of school) as Becky has shown.

So how can we best use AI mediated video formats for instructional design?

#### **1. Procedural-motor content. **

[Höffler and Leutner’s 2007 meta-analysis ](https://substack.com/redirect/98ff3f4c-8166-4cb9-a689-85b0cec790e6?j=eyJ1IjoiMXJ6YXV2In0.i_L8wvFkLUcXdz4pbEPfM8vw56XPotyGsyX7YQYkvWs)found that animation outperforms static pictures with d = 1.06 specifically for procedural-motor content where the learning objective involves spatial transformation over time. Many YouTube tutorials are exactly this: how to fix a tap, how to truss a chicken, how to perform a free-throw, how to wire a plug. For procedural-motor learning, video has a real and well-documented advantage, and short-form is often the right length.

#### **2. Vocabulary and single-fact acquisition with repetition.**

Microlearning at the TikTok scale can deliver discrete facts repeatedly enough to embed them, particularly in language-learning contexts where the learner returns to the same vocabulary repeatedly. This is the strongest empirical case in the short-form literature, though even here the longitudinal evidence is thin so far.

#### **3. Curiosity ignition **

The format is unusually effective at producing the moment of *“I want to know more about this,”* which is a precondition for sustained engagement with a topic. This is a real cognitive good. A child who watches a Mark Rober video about marble runs is arguably more likely to investigate physics than a child who has not. The literature on interest and motivation in learning supports this; the precondition is real.

#### 4. Exposure to topics that would not otherwise be encountered.

The discovery surface of YouTube and TikTok is wide. People learn that quantum entanglement, octopus cognition, the structure of the bond market, or the history of the Antikythera mechanism exist as topics worth thinking about. This is genuine information enrichment, even if the depth is shallow.

#### 5. Affective relationship with subject matter.

A child who develops parasocial warmth towards Hank Green is more likely to identify with science as a domain that is interesting, accessible, and culturally legitimate. The empirical case for this as a learning effect per se is weak, but its role as a precondition for later learning is plausible.

None of these are nothing. None of them are the same as “students could learn more in 5 hours of video than 8 hours of school.” They are, at the very least, the conditions that prepare the ground for learning to take place elsewhere.

## Closing

Educational video, in the form most students now consume it, has run ahead of the science designed to evaluate it. The field is in roughly the position medicine would be in if the only randomised trials had been conducted on a population that no longer existed. The principles are good. The patient has changed. Designers, parents and educators have to make decisions every day about whether to accept the medium as it now is, or whether to push back against the conditions that have made it less educative than it could be. I do not think those decisions can wait for the literature to catch up. They are being made by default, by those with the budgets to commission video and the algorithms to distribute it, and the burden of pushing back falls on those of us who still think learning is a more demanding cognitive activity than the current medium is willing to acknowledge.
