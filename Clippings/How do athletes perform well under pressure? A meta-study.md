## ABSTRACT

Many studies have explored athlete performance under pressure, with recent emphases on choking prevention and definitions of clutch performance. Whilst these two areas may benefit sport performance research, it may also be important to explore the prominent factors in facilitating athlete performance under pressure to promote the development of interventions targeting clutch performance in the future. In the present review, we synthesised qualitative research on athlete performance under pressure to highlight the psychological factors and strategies useful in facilitating performance under pressure in sport. Given the difficulties in reviewing and conducting quantitative research on these concepts (e.g. there currently is no validated measure of pressure), we chose to review qualitative research. Database searchers were conducted in September 2022, August 2023, and August 2024. This resulted in the identification and meta-synthesis of 29 articles reporting qualitative findings. Athletes, coaches, and sporting practitioners considered self-efficacy, demand appraisal, mental toughness, and task-focused resource allocation as important in facilitating athlete performance under pressure. Pre-performance routines, imagery techniques, self-talk, goal setting, cognitive restructuring, and simulated pressure training were also reported as useful strategies to facilitate athlete performance under pressure.

Performance under pressure is a core aspect of high-performance sport, and understanding how to facilitate performance in pressured situations is a key role of sport psychology practitioners (Low et al., [Citation2023](#)). A large majority of researchers have adopted Baumeister’s ([Citation1984](#)) definition of pressure, which is premised as athletes’ perceptions of increased incentives to perform well (e.g. Low et al., [Citation2023](#); Otten, [Citation2009](#); Schweickle et al., [Citation2021a](#), [Citation2021b](#)). Often, these perceptions can precede either a clutch performance, or episode of choking, during an event. On the back of a series of studies exploring definitional issues (e.g. Schweickle et al., [Citation2021a](#), [Citation2021b](#), [Citation2022](#)), clutch performance has most recently been defined as ‘the extent to which athletes achieve their self-referenced goals under an increased appraisal of pressure’ (Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#), p. 8). Choking, meanwhile, has commonly been premised on a performance decrement under perceived pressure (Mesagno & Hill, [Citation2013](#)). Although the outcomes of these constructs broadly sit at opposing ends of a performance spectrum (i.e. performing well or poorly under pressure), the underlying processes of occurrence of clutch performance and choking are distinct (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)). As such, athletes can respond positively or negatively to pressure, with potentially different processes underlying these different performance responses.

Choking in sport has received a great deal of attention in recent decades, with researchers focusing on the underlying causes, experience, and potential strategies to reduce the likelihood of choking. Prior research has suggested that fear of failure (Gucciardi et al., [Citation2010](#)), emotion-focused attention (Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#)) or loss of emotional control (Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)), and negative interpretations of situational demands (i.e. perceiving the situational demands as outside of the athlete’s capabilities; Hill et al., [Citation2009](#)) all underlie the occurrence of choking in sport. As such, a number of qualitative (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)) and mixed-methods (e.g. Mesagno et al., [Citation2008](#)) studies have been focused on the impact of strategies that may minimise these factors, to reduce the likelihood of choking. As a result, there are several established theories underpinning choking (see Mesagno & Beckmann, [Citation2017](#)), and interventions implemented to reduce the likelihood of choking in sport (see Gröpel & Mesagno, [Citation2019](#)).

Clutch performance has received less attention regarding the underlying processes of occurrence, and has often been investigated alongside the experience of choking (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)). Of the research exploring these processes, athletes have reported task-focused attention (Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)), high confidence (Maher et al., [Citation2020](#)), and high mental toughness (Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)) to contribute to their ability to excel under pressure. Additionally, Swann and colleagues ([Citation2017](#)) explored the potential psychological state underlying clutch performance (i.e. clutch states), noting the state to be characterised by a number of factors, such as complete focus, intense effort, and increased confidence.

Whilst there are several established theories and interventions regarding choking in sport, the lack of investigation into clutch performance has hindered the development of clear, explanatory frameworks and efficacious interventions targeting the facilitation of performance under pressure (see Schweickle et al., [Citation2020](#)). Indeed, previous literature has outlined the need for researchers to focus on the mechanisms underlying clutch performance to benefit the development of strategies targeted at facilitating performance under pressure (e.g. Hufton et al., [Citation2023](#); Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#)). To date, however, there has been no review bringing together the specific factors and strategies that facilitate performance under pressure in sport. One of the issues in synthesising this research has been the range of constructs utilised to refer to similar standards of performance. For example, researchers have explored clutch performance (e.g. Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#)), effective coping (Nicholls et al., [Citation2005](#)), clutch moments (e.g. Schweickle et al., [Citation2021a](#), [Citation2021b](#)), improved or superior performance (e.g. Low et al., [Citation2023](#)), clutch states (e.g. Swann et al., [Citation2017](#)), and choking resistance (e.g. Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#)) to describe how athletes can either excel, or maintain their performance, under pressure (both of which have previously been referred to as clutch performance; e.g. Hibbs, [Citation2010](#)). As such, there remains a risk that by reviewing research exclusively using a singular term (e.g. clutch performance), important information is excluded that reports on the same phenomenon solely on the basis of using different terminology (i.e. jangle fallacy; Credé, [Citation2018](#)). Additionally, given the difficulties in replicating pressure in lab settings (Kent et al., [Citation2018](#)), accompanied by the fact that there are no validated measures of pressure to utilise in sports settings, a review of quantitative research may be problematic. To address these issues, a meta-synthesis of qualitative research inclusive of the different constructs used to capture athlete performance under pressure may provide a useful understanding of how athletes can facilitate performance during these instances of high pressure. This could allow for prominent themes and ideas regarding athlete performance under pressure to be identified (e.g. Thorne et al., [Citation2004](#)), regardless of the specific conceptual framing.

### The current study

Given the difficulties mentioned above in conducting a systematic review of quantitative literature on performance under pressure, a meta-synthesis of qualitative research is an appropriate option to effectively collate and review the current literature. In reviewing the current literature in the field of performance pressure, a foundation of knowledge from which researchers can build to develop theory, measurement, and interventions could be provided. Additionally, the findings may complement prior literature exploring the benefits of practicing under pressure (e.g. Low et al., [Citation2023](#)) by identifying the key factors and strategies involved in facilitating successful performance under pressure. As such, the aim of the present study was to review and meta-synthesise the existing qualitative literature that focused on performance under pressure in sport. Specifically, this review aimed to address the following research questions: (a) what factors facilitate, or inhibit, athlete performance under pressure?; and (b) how can athlete performance under pressure be improved? In turn, this review can contribute to the field by providing a synthesis of qualitative work on performance pressure in sport and inform future development of strategies to facilitate performance under pressure.

## Methods

### Meta-study approach

A meta-study approach was adopted to synthesise qualitative literature on performance under pressure in sport. Specifically, meta-study involves analysing the theory, method, and results of qualitative research to generate new insight into a phenomenon (Paterson et al., [Citation2001](#)). In adopting a meta-study approach, Paterson and colleagues ([Citation2001](#)) outlined six steps to follow: (1) formation of research team, theoretical lens, and objectives; (2) identification of existing literature; (3) categorisation of data based on pre-selected criteria; (4) analysis of methodologies; (5) examination of theory; and (6) synthesis of ideas, directions, and implications for future research. In using a meta-study approach, researchers can gain insight into current issues with theory and philosophical positioning in the field, alongside the qualitative results regarding the phenomenon of interest. As such, this review will synthesise the broad qualitative findings pertaining to athlete performance under pressure, as well as the methodologies, philosophies, and guiding theories used in performance under pressure literature. In conducting this review, we followed the guidelines for enhancing transparency in reporting the synthesis of qualitative research (ENTREQ) (Tong et al., [Citation2012](#)).

In synthesising qualitative research, Williams and Shaw ([Citation2016](#)) note researchers can adopt either an interpretative or integrative approach to research. Whilst interpretative approaches are useful in exploring existing literature, and generating new theory, researchers utilising an integrative approach seek to answer questions in the literature and test pre-existing theory. It is important, however, to view these methods on a continuum, as opposed to entirely separate methods, as any one meta-synthesis may incorporate characteristics of both approaches (Williams & Shaw, [Citation2016](#)). Given the current review was rather exploratory in nature, with an aim to understand the common themes among performance under pressure literature to inform future theory development, we approached the research from a largely interpretative position. That is to say, we acknowledge that the findings will be influenced by our prior experiences, and as a result, the final synthesis will reflect our interpretations of the results reported across the literature base.

In conducting this review, we followed a pragmatic approach to research. Ontologically, pragmatists argue that ‘a continuum exists between objective and subjective viewpoints’ (Giacobbi et al., [Citation2005](#), p. 22), where the nature of the research questions and process can vary where the researcher is positioned. Given our focus on subjective experiences (i.e. participant perspectives of performance under pressure and researcher interpretations of the data), we remain largely situated within a subjective viewpoint. Whilst we seek a greater understanding of athlete performance under pressure to better inform future interventions, we also recognise the subjectivity present in the reviewed literature. Epistemologically, we acknowledge that information we gain is relative (Feilzer, [Citation2010](#)), and thus, it is important to be reflexive in the interpretation and analysis of data. In considering this, we recognise the potentially different understandings of what contributes to athlete performance under pressure based on the varying socially constructed views present in the reviewed literature.

### Criteria for study inclusion

Studies were eligible for inclusion in the review if they: (a) were a peer-reviewed journal article published in English; (b) report original qualitative research; and, (c) as a main aim of the study, explored the perceptions of athletes or relevant stakeholders (e.g. coaches, sport psychologists) on athlete performance under pressure in sport. Articles were excluded if they: (a) explored performance under pressure in a context outside of sport; or, (b) used only quantitative methods to assess performance under pressure (articles using mixed-methods of data analysis were included for review if they met the inclusion criteria).

### Literature search and screening process

To identify relevant databases for our literature search, we referred to previous reviews on similar topics (e.g. Schweickle et al., [Citation2020](#)) and based our decision on the databases we had access to. As such, we used the following online databases to find relevant studies for review: APA PsycInfo, Academic Search Complete, Medline, SPORTDiscus, CINAHL Plus, APA PsycArticles, and PubMed. In line with the SPIDER framework (Cooke et al., [Citation2012](#)), preliminary search terms were developed by the authors, and trialled using the EBSCOhost database (see Supplementary File 3). These initial searches were reviewed and repeated until an effective combination of search terms was identified (i.e. a search strategy that can identify all relevant studies) (Siddaway et al., [Citation2019](#)). The final search combination was: (perform\* OR chok\* OR clutch) AND (sport\* OR athlet\* OR coach\*) AND (qualitativ\* OR them\* OR interview\* OR categor\* OR ‘mixed method\*’) AND (pressur\* OR anxi\* OR stress\*). Following the preliminary database searches, the first formal search was conducted in September 2022, where the first and third author imported and independently screened articles according to their title, abstract, and keywords (i.e. each author screened all texts, and agreed on which texts will be included). During this process, Covidence software was used to manage the records, and duplicate articles were automatically removed. In screening articles at the full text level, the first author independently screened all texts, whilst the second, third, and fourth authors screened the articles in duplicate (i.e. a third each). For an article to be included in the review, authors were required to come to a consensus (i.e. two authors agreed whether the text should be included or excluded). The level of agreement at both stages of screening was near-perfect (*κ* =.96 at the title, abstract, and keywords stage of screening; *κ*  =.95 at the full text stage). In cases where two authors disagreed on the eligibility of an article, the case was discussed with the remaining authors before making the final decision. Additionally, the first and third authors conducted a second search of databases in August 2023, and fourth search in August 2024, where both authors independently screened all texts according to the title, abstract, and keywords, followed by screening at full text in the same manner (i.e. articles were included after a consensus was reached). Finally, the first author conducted a backward citation search on all studies included for review, alongside a manual search through Google Scholar.

### Data extraction

The first author extracted data from relevant studies using a data extraction spreadsheet. In instances where results or other information were ambiguous, authors discussed during peer debrief sessions. Data we were interested in included citation information, setting (i.e. country the study was conducted in), aims of the study, method and design, sample information (e.g. sample size, age, expertise), methodology, method of analysis, guiding theory, philosophical position, sampling strategy, method of data collection, techniques to improve rigour, and results from each study (i.e. themes, key concepts, and example quotes).

### Analysis

A four-stage meta-study approach was utilised in this review, consisting of meta-data analysis, meta-method analysis, meta-theory analysis followed by a meta-synthesis (Paterson et al., [Citation2001](#)). The final meta-synthesis was conducted after the first three analytic stages, as the synthesis is derived from the results generated during these steps.

Our approach to meta-data analysis was guided by Thomas and Harden’s ([Citation2008](#)) thematic synthesis. This involved three stages: (1) coding extracted data; (2) organising related codes into descriptive themes; and (3) developing analytical themes. This process began with openly coding the extracted data based on its meaning and content. Following this, and in many cases overlapping with the first stage, we developed descriptive themes to summarise the findings (e.g. preparation is important to performance). These descriptive themes were then organised into higher level analytical themes as the coding process continued, providing new insight into the topic (e.g. explaining the mechanism underlying preparation, and how it can facilitate athlete performance under pressure). During this process, the first author led the initial coding, however, trustworthiness was developed through research team members acting as critical friends and engaging in regular peer debriefing (i.e. reviewing and challenging findings; Smith & McGannon, [Citation2018](#)).

Meta-method analysis involves recording details of procedures (e.g. methods of sampling and data collection), rigour, and soundness of the methods used in primary research studies (Paterson et al., [Citation2001](#); Ronkainen et al., [Citation2022](#)). In doing so, we can determine the appropriateness of specific methods in exploring phenomena of interest and compare the implications of different methodologies (Ronkainen et al., [Citation2022](#)). To evaluate the methods used in qualitative performance under pressure literature, the following criteria guided the meta-method analysis: study purpose; setting; methodology; sample; sampling strategy; methods of data collection; data analysis techniques; and methods of enhancing rigour.

Meta-theory analysis involves critiquing the philosophical position and chosen theory underpinning primary research studies, and considering the implications these perspectives may have on other aspects of the research (Paterson et al., [Citation2001](#)). Whilst providing a good indication of researcher positionality within a particular field, meta-theory analysis can also contribute to a more informed understanding of how theory is applied to research on a specific phenomenon (Ronkainen et al., [Citation2022](#)).

The final meta-synthesis brings together the analysis of the steps mentioned above, to produce a new insight on the phenomenon (Ronkainen et al., [Citation2022](#)). This is particularly useful in providing insight into the implications of varying philosophical, theoretical, and methodological issues, and informing future research of appropriate methods to continue investigation on a specific phenomenon, as well as the specific aspects to continue exploration in.

### Quality appraisal

The CASP appraisal tool (CASP, [Citation2014](#)) was used by the first and third author to assess for quality of included studies (i.e. 20% of studies were assessed in duplicate). Whilst we acknowledge that qualitative research checklists (e.g. the CASP tool) prioritise technical procedures over less observable study attributes that may impose a larger influence, and may also become outdated over time (i.e. criteria can change over time when new critiques become apparent) (Tod et al., [Citation2022](#)), we opted to use this tool for several reasons. Namely, the CASP tool is a good measure of research practice transparency and provides a good indication regarding procedural aspects of a study (Long et al., [Citation2020](#)). Additionally, the CASP tool is the most commonly used quality appraisal tool in qualitative syntheses of health-related research (Long et al., [Citation2020](#)) and has also been used in prior sport-focused meta-studies (e.g. Sutcliffe et al., [Citation2021](#)).

### Meta-method analysis

A comprehensive overview of each study’s methodological approach is provided in Supplementary File 1. The included studies were conducted in the United Kingdom (*n* = 15), Australia (*n*  = 10), Ireland (*n*  = 1), Germany (*n* = 1), Japan (*n* = 1), and the Netherlands (*n*  = 1). The most common methodologies reported included using a mixed-method design (*n*  = 7) and phenomenological approach (*n*  = 5), whilst action research, experience cycle methodology and a grounded theory methodology were all reported once. The remaining 14 studies did not report any specific methodology.

There were 543 participants contributing to the qualitative data detailed in this review, consisting of athletes (*n*  = 453), coaches (*n*  = 77), and practitioners (*n*  = 13). Participants were involved in 45 different sports, with soccer, golf, basketball, variations of running, and variations of rugby league being the most reported. Athlete performance standards ranged from recreational to world-class elite (i.e. athletes that experience sustained success at the highest level in their sport; Swann et al., [Citation2015](#)). Several studies did not report the range of participant ages, however, of the studies that did report this, participant ages ranged from 14–68 years. Whilst the gender of 32 participants was not reported, the sample consisted of both male (*n* = 353) and female (*n*  = 158) participants. Nine studies did not report on the specific sampling strategy adopted; however, they did provide enough detail for the authors to infer. Of the studies that did explicitly mention the use of a sampling strategy, 10 studies utilised purposive sampling as the sole method of participant recruitment. The remaining articles utilised a combination of both purposive and snowball sampling (*n*  = 8), with one article reporting the use of convenience sampling (Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)) and one study utilising intensity sampling (Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)).

The majority of studies included for review utilised varying forms of individual interviews (e.g. event-focused; Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)) as the sole method of qualitative data collection (*n*  = 24). Some authors also chose to combine methods and incorporate focus groups (*n* = 2; Gucciardi et al., [Citation2010](#); Hanton et al., [Citation2005](#)), as well as a reflective diary (*n*  = 1; Hill et al., [Citation2011](#)). Additionally, two studies used surveys with open ended questions for data collection (Hirsch et al., [Citation2024](#); Roberts et al., [Citation2022](#)). The most common approa­ches to qualitative data analysis include forms of content analysis (i.e. directed, inductive, deductive; *n*  = 8) and thematic analysis (*n*  = 9). Other methods of data analysis included a grounded theory approach to coding (e.g. Gucciardi et al., [Citation2010](#)), various forms of phenomenological analysis (e.g. reduction, interpretative) (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2017](#)), the Kawakita Jiro method (Endo et al., [Citation2023](#)), and establishing causal networks (Hanton & Connaughton, [Citation2002](#)). All studies included for review made attempts to improve rigour. The most commonly utilised method was through a multiple team member approach (i.e. peer debrief, critical friends, author consensus on findings; *n*  = 20). Other common methods included member checking (*n*  = 14), bracketing (*n*  = 4), providing thick description (*n*  = 4), and utilising consensus validation procedures (*n*  = 6).

### Qualitative research checklist

Results of our CASP analysis can be found in the supplemental materials. All studies reported an explicit aim, utilised qualitative methods appropriately, justified their use of research design, used appropriate methods of data collection, and reported clear findings. Eight studies did not give an explicit name to their recruitment strategy (see Supplementary File 1), however, enough information was provided to infer that all participant samples were appropriate to address study aims. Some studies were not sufficiently rigorous (97% of studies discussed analysis in detail), did not completely consider ethical issues (93% of studies sought ethical approval and/or participant consent), and did not adequately consider the relationship between participant and researcher (86% of studies made a sufficient attempt to reduce or acknowledge potential researcher bias). The lack of consideration noted here for ethical issues (i.e. participant consent and ethical approval) and the participant-researcher relationship is of particular concern and researchers should aim to ensure these issues are addressed in future.

### Meta-theory analysis

The majority of studies did not report a specific theoretical framework as part of their approach (*n*  = 20). The remaining studies were underpinned by self-focus models (*n*  = 1; Mesagno et al., [Citation2009](#)), distraction models (*n*  = 1; Mesagno et al., [Citation2008](#)), both self-focus and distraction models (*n*  = 1; Ellis & Ward, [Citation2022](#)), self-presentation models (*n*  = 1; Hill et al., [Citation2017](#)), skill acquisition theory (*n*  = 1; Cormack & Gillman, [Citation2021](#)), personal construct psychology (*n*  = 1; Gucciardi et al., [Citation2010](#)), transactional process perspective (*n*  = 1; Nicholls et al., [Citation2005](#)), implementation intention theory (*n*  = 1; Hirsch et al., [Citation2024](#)), and the contingence-competence-control model (*n*  = 1; Jordet et al., [Citation2006](#)). The concept of choking was explored in 12 studies (including *n*  = 1 exploring choking resistance), with variations of clutch performance (e.g. clutch states) being explored in six studies. Whilst less common, studies also explored coping effectiveness (e.g. Nicholls et al., [Citation2005](#)), thought processes under pressure (e.g. Roberts et al., [Citation2022](#)), and improved under pressure (e.g. Low et al., [Citation2023](#)). Additionally, whilst Baumeister’s ([Citation1984](#)) definition of pressure was the most used definition among included studies, some researchers conceptualised pressure as performance-related stress (e.g. Nicholls et al., [Citation2005](#)) or explored the relationship between performance and symptoms of anxiety, where pressure was considered to play a key role (e.g. Hanton & Connaughton, [Citation2002](#)).

Regarding the approach adopted by studies in this review, most studies did not specify their philosophical position (*n*  = 16). Of the studies that did specify this, critical realism was the most common approach (*n*  = 5), with other perspectives including pragmatism (*n*  = 2), relativism (*n*  = 2), social constructionism (*n*  = 1), constructivism (*n*  = 1), essentialism (*n* = 1), and interpretivism (*n*  = 1).

The results from this analysis were grouped into themes of athlete performance under pressure, with these themes providing an outline of factors and strategies reported to facilitate performance. These themes include: (a) athlete performance under pressure can be facilitated through several individual factors; and (b) athletes can use cognitive–behavioural strategies to enhance important individual factors.

### Athlete performance under pressure can be facilitated through several individual factors

Athletes, coaches, and sport psychologists noted athlete performance under pressure to be facilitated by a number of individual factors (i.e. primarily internal beliefs and states that may directly influence athlete performance under pressure). Most notably, an athlete’s ability to positively appraise their situational demands, athlete self-efficacy, the resources the athlete has available to allocate towards the task, and an athlete’s mental toughness were all suggested to be significant contributors to athlete performance under pressure.

### Allocating resources to the task (focus, attention, effort, preparation)

The most common theme acknowledged by studies regards the resources allocated by the athlete to their task (*n*  = 25). Increased focus (e.g. Maher et al., [Citation2020](#)), task-oriented attention (e.g. Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#)), effort (e.g. Swann et al., [Citation2017](#)), and thorough preparation (e.g. Hanton et al., [Citation2005](#)) all contributed to athlete performance under pressure. Specifically, increased levels of focus were associated with episodes of clutch performance, with emphasis on the deliberate and effortful nature, as one athlete noted: ‘It’s very much a conscious effort to really focus … a conscious effort really to make myself play better’ (Swann et al., [Citation2017](#), p. 2275). The subject of focus was also important, as Hill et al. ([Citation2011](#)) reported that focusing on the processes required for skill execution (e.g. holistic swing feel for a golf shot), as opposed to the emotions experienced by the athlete (e.g. anxiety) or the potential outcome of the event (i.e. win or loss), may benefit athletes in facilitating performance under pressure. Additionally, adequate preparation was considered as crucial, given the reduction in stress and unexpected situations for athletes during competition, as one golfer noted: ‘I have to have everything sorted … I haven’t got anything to get stressed about. I will try and prepare as much as I can, to take the stress out of the day’ (Hill et al., [Citation2010](#), p. 227). Equally, decreased focus, emotion-focused attention, and inadequate preparation were considered to encourage episodes of choking. As such, to facilitate performance under pressure, athletes should make efforts to minimise the likelihood of unexpected stressors (e.g. through simulated practice; Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)) and implement strategies to promote task-focused attention (e.g. pre-performance routines; Hill et al., [Citation2011](#)).

### Self-efficacy

A common finding across the literature was the pivotal role that self-efficacy plays in performing under pressure. An athlete’s confidence in their ability to achieve their goal (e.g. Maher et al., [Citation2020](#)), the expectations they hold (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2017](#)), and the level of perceived control they have over their actions and emotions (e.g. Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#); Schweickle et al., [Citation2021a](#), [Citation2021b](#)), were all reported to contribute to an athlete’s ability to perform under pressure (*n*  = 20 studies noted the importance of these factors). For example, one basketball athlete claimed: ‘It’s about self-confidence and believing in yourself’ (Maher et al., [Citation2020](#), p. 427), when attempting a free-throw under pressure. Importantly, these factors may be interrelated, as confidence was seen to underpin some perceptions of perceived control (e.g. Jordet et al., [Citation2006](#)) and athlete expectations (e.g. Swann et al., [Citation2017](#)). Additionally, whilst high self-efficacy was frequently associated with episodes of clutch performance, low self-efficacy was also considered to be a key factor contributing to episodes of choking. Thus, to facilitate performance under pressure, athletes should seek to implement strategies that enhance self-efficacy. Specifically, athletes can implement approach coping strategies (e.g. pre-performance routine, simulated practice), imagery techniques, and set realistic, process-related goals prior to the event.

### Facilitative appraisal of demands

The appraisal of situational demands was noted by 19 studies to contribute to an athlete’s ability to perform under pressure. Appraising anxiety as facilitative, and appraising the situation as a challenge, was suggested to facilitate athlete performance under pressure (e.g. Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#)). On the other hand, appraising anxiety as debilitative, and appraising the situational demands as outside the capabilities of the athlete, was noted to encourage choking, as a tennis athlete recalled their choking episode: ‘This anxiety comes up to you … It doesn't let you move, doesn't let you blink. It over-powers everything and destroys any confidence you've built’ (Hill et al., [Citation2017](#), p. 145). This appraisal was also considered as partly dependent on athlete self-efficacy and mental toughness (i.e. self-efficacy and mental toughness are associated with challenge appraisals) (Hill et al., [Citation2017](#); Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)). As such, to facilitate performance under pressure, athletes should seek to reframe their appraisal of demanding situations (see cognitive restructuring below) and improve individual factors that may underpin this appraisal (i.e. self-efficacy and mental toughness).

### Mental toughness

Five studies highlighted the significance of mental toughness in performing under pressure. Low mental toughness was associated with instances of choking, whilst higher levels were linked to excelling under pressure (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)). This trend was also acknowledged by sport practitioners, where low mental toughness was associated with a predisposition to choking, as one participant suggested: ‘Is someone predisposed to choke? Yes … because they are lacking in some of these \[mental toughness, self-confidence, and functional thinking\] key variables’ (Hill et al., [Citation2009](#), p. 208). Participants also spoke to the high levels of mental toughness displayed by world class athletes when performing under pressure, noting that high mental toughness helps athletes focus on the task (Maher et al., [Citation2020](#)). Additionally, athletes high in mental toughness were seen to appraise pressure situations as a challenge, alongside enhanced focus and an improved ability to quickly develop solutions to event-related problems, facilitating the transition into a clutch state (Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)). As such, athletes should seek to improve their mental toughness, to help prevent choking, and facilitate clutch performance.

### Athletes can use cognitive-behavioural strategies to enhance important individual factors

A range of cognitive and behavioural strategies were reported to both facilitate athlete performance and reduce the likelihood of choking under pressure. These are distinct from the individual factors mentioned above, in that they are specific strategies that the athlete can employ. This contrasts to self-efficacy, for example, which is often considered as an individual’s belief in their ability to complete a task (Schwarzer & Luszczynska, [Citation2008](#)). Athletes can implement these mental techniques and behaviours to improve specific individual factors, often resulting in the facilitation of athlete performance under pressure. These strategies include pre-performance routines, simulated practice, imagery techniques, self-talk, goal setting, and cognitive restructuring.

#### Pre-performance routine

Ten studies noted the importance of a pre-performance routine in contributing to athlete performance under pressure. Whilst this strategy was primarily utilised among athletes participating in self-paced sports (e.g. golf, ten-pin bowling) to promote a fluid execution of a skill under pressure (e.g. Mesagno et al., [Citation2008](#)), some findings suggested a pre-performance routine may also be useful to prepare for racing events (e.g. swimming; Hanton et al., [Citation2007](#)), and in more reactive sporting environments (e.g. netball; Hill et al., [Citation2017](#)). Pre-performance routines were most often utilised to prevent an athlete from choking, due to the benefit they have in reducing arousal in high-pressure situations (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2011](#)) and ensuring consistency in the execution of skills (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)). For example, one coach noted: ‘You give them a set routine, and they do the same all the time … your body has then been trained and you press the button and you hit the golf ball … if you keep doing that then it will block out all other things’ (Hill et al., [Citation2010](#), p. 231). Additionally, pre-performance routines were reported to provide athletes with an opportunity to implement mental skills (e.g. positive self-talk; Maher et al., [Citation2020](#)), aiding in the reduction of negative self-talk, providing a distraction from the crowd during skill execution, and reducing athlete self-awareness (Mesagno et al., [Citation2008](#)). In turn, these routines benefit athletes by reducing their likelihood of choking under pressure.

#### Simulated practice

Seven studies acknowledged the contribution that simulated practice has on athlete performance under pressure. Indeed, training under pressurised conditions, or in situations similar to the expected event, may help improve athlete confidence, promote focus, and encourage management of anxiety during pressurised events (Hill et al., [Citation2011](#)). This strategy was especially useful in reducing the likelihood of choking under pressure (e.g. Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)), potentially due to the benefit it provides in reducing unexpected stressors for athletes and increasing familiarity of the situation. Athletes who excelled under pressure also suggested that simulated practice facilitates execution of skills when under pressure, as one golfer noted: ‘I put myself in so many impossible situations … If I then put myself in that situation on the course by accident, I can say … watch me! I am going to get up and down from here’ (Hill et al., [Citation2010](#), p. 227).

#### Imagery techniques

The use of cognitive strategies, such as imagery and mental rehearsal, was reported in nine studies. This strategy was often utilised prior to the execution of a specific skill (e.g. a golf shot) to visualise the target, or the type of shot the athlete wanted to make (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)). The use of imagery techniques was noted to help athletes obtain a facilitative interpretation of anxiety-related symptoms, increase self-confidence, and improve perceived control over skill execution (Hanton et al., [Citation2007](#); Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)). The use of this strategy was reported by athletes to be useful in the prevention of choking (e.g. Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)), and especially important in facilitating successful performance under pressure, due to the benefit it has in improving perceived control and self-confidence (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#); Hill et al., [Citation2013](#)). For example, one athlete recalled: ‘I imagined myself in the situation, coping with it, and putting better … I also tried to imagine how I felt under pressure in my normal sport and how I coped with that situation … It worked well’ (Hill et al., [Citation2013](#), p. 321). On the other hand, adverse effects and choking were reported when using this strategy inappropriately (e.g. without adequate supervision or teaching) or when the athlete was unable to maintain control of their image (Hill et al., [Citation2010](#); Maher et al., [Citation2020](#)).

#### Self-talk

Ten studies acknowledged the importance of self-talk in helping athletes cope with pressure. Positive and task-oriented self-talk was often considered to facilitate performance under pressure, as it can protect athlete self-confidence, aid in the appraisal of anxiety, and promote the regaining of task-focused attention following an error made by the athlete (Hill et al., [Citation2009](#); Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#)). This is evident in a quote provided by a choking-resistant athlete: ‘I was using (positive) self-talk, and no real distracters were there and didn’t really notice the audience after that’ (Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#), p. 81). Negative self-talk, however, was consistently associated with a breakdown in athlete performance under pressure, potentially due to the distraction it offers from task-related cognitions (e.g. Mesagno et al., [Citation2008](#)). As such, reducing negative self-talk may benefit an athlete in mitigating the likelihood of choking, whilst encouraging positive and task-oriented self-talk may help facilitate successful performance under pressure.

#### Goal setting

The importance of goal setting was noted by 11 studies. Specifically, goal setting was not only suggested to be an effective strategy to prevent choking and facilitate clutch (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2009](#)), but goals were also important in the assessment of clutch performance for athletes and coaches (e.g. Hufton et al., [Citation2023](#); Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#)). Authors noted that setting process-related goals that were within the athlete’s control increased task-focused attention, and may also improve perceived control (Hill et al., [Citation2009](#); Hill et al., [Citation2017](#); Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)). Additionally, the setting of specific goals (i.e. objective, measurable) was noted to precede the occurrence of clutch states (Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)). Whilst outcome-oriented goals were often considered to inhibit performance when the athlete felt their goal was threatened (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2009](#)), one participant noted becoming: ‘very outcome oriented’ in the process of achieving a clutch state (Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#), p. 9). As such, whilst the use of process-oriented goals was commonly reported to reduce the likelihood of choking under pressure, it may be appropriate for athletes to vary their goal-setting strategy based on the situational demands and contextual factors present. Additionally, athletes reported their goals to change throughout an event in response to changing situational demands (Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#)), indicating that athletes have the capability to adjust their goal-setting strategy within competitive events.

#### Cognitive restructuring

The utilisation of cognitive restructuring was seen to be an effective strategy for athletes performing under pressure in eight studies. The restructuring of thoughts and cognitions regarding situational demands was suggested to aid athletes in coping with the pressure they experienced, prevent choking, and facilitate performance (Hanton et al., [Citation2007](#); Hill et al., [Citation2011](#); Hill & Hemmings, [Citation2014](#)). For example, one golfer recalled utilising cognitive restructuring during a clutch performance: ‘I suppose I thought about the situation differently, turned it into something I could manage, and then just got on with it, and hit it close’ (Hill & Hemmings, [Citation2014](#), p. 530). Negative cognitions (e.g. perceiving situational demands as outside athlete capabilities) were often considered to contribute to choking under pressure (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)), and as such, the restructuring of these cognitions was reported to help in the prevention of choking (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)), and benefit athletes in effectively coping with pressure (Nicholls et al., [Citation2005](#)). To then facilitate performance under pressure, athletes were reported to restructure these cognitions as positive (e.g. perceiving situational demands as within athlete capabilities), thereby facilitating performance (e.g. Hill & Hemmings, [Citation2014](#)). This reappraisal was noted to occur alongside positive self-talk, and increases in task-focused attention, resulting in the athlete reframing the situation as positive (Hill et al., [Citation2010](#); Nicholls et al., [Citation2005](#)).

### Meta-synthesis

This meta-synthesis represents data from 29 studies and 543 coaches, athletes, and sporting practitioners. To synthesise the data, however, it is first important to be aware of potential issues present in the literature regarding the adoption of varying philosophical perspectives and guiding theories. Specifically, many studies did not report a guiding theory or their philosophical perspective. Whilst reasonable that theory was not reported in all studies given the exploratory nature of the research, especially studies investigating clutch performance, the absence of philosophical perspective evident in many studies is of concern. Outlining a philosophical perspective in any research, particularly qualitative, is important to provide the reader with an understanding of how the results of data analysis were interpreted (Kawulich, [Citation2016](#)). In addition, there are some concerns regarding philosophical and methodological coherence among reviewed studies (*n*  = 4 studies appeared to present issues in coherence). For example, some studies implemented strategies to reduce researcher bias (i.e. member checking), despite the contradictions evident in methods of data analysis (i.e. reflexive thematic analysis) and philosophical positioning (e.g. constructivism, interpretivism) (Braun & Clarke, [Citation2023](#); Motulsky, [Citation2021](#); Smith, [Citation2008](#)). Given these issues, there is a need for future research to more consistently outline their philosophical position and adopt methods of data analysis that align with this perspective.

Through mapping the different levels of results (i.e. codes, descriptive themes, analytical themes; Thomas & Harden, [Citation2008](#)), we offer a descriptive model of the findings (see Figure 2). Figure 2 outlines how strategies (e.g. pre-performance routines) influence individual factors (e.g. attention), resulting in an influence on perceived performance under pressure. In some cases, this relationship may be considered bidirectional, as some individual factors reported were suggested to influence the effectiveness of strategies implemented by athletes. For example, Hill et al. ([Citation2010](#)) noted the implementation of imagery within an event to be dependent on an athlete’s ability to control the image (i.e. perceived control influences the effectiveness of this strategy). Additionally, prior performance (i.e. any experience the athlete has had previously within a similar sporting event) may have an impact on some individual factors, as athletes reported prior performances to influence their confidence in their ability to perform in the subsequent event (e.g. Hanton et al., [Citation2007](#); Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)). For example, an athlete successfully executing a skill in a similar competitive environment prior to the current event, will likely improve their confidence in replicating the skill under pressure. However, whilst we attempted to provide an in-depth overview of how athlete performance under pressure may be facilitated in the descriptive model (Figure 2), it should be noted that it may not capture the complete nature of the relationships discussed. This is because several of the strategies presented may both precede, and occur as a result of, the individual factors noted above. There may also be a relationship between strategies, where the use of a specific strategy may influence the effectiveness of other strategies (e.g. using self-talk alongside cognitive restructuring). To further refine these findings, it may be beneficial for future research to explicitly investigate the relationships between cognitive–behavioural strategies, and the individual factors highlighted in this review.

Given the influence that the individual factors and strategies mentioned above exert on each other, it is important to note how these strategies can be targeted to facilitate performance under pressure. These findings suggest that whilst specific strategies may enhance certain factors, these individual factors may also moderate the effectiveness of the strategies being implemented by the athlete. For example, Jackman et al. ([Citation2020](#)) noted that confident athletes may more effectively implement positive self-talk during an event, whilst Hill et al. ([Citation2010](#)) reported athletes with low perceived control were unable to effectively utilise imagery techniques. Athletes should therefore consider their own individual factors before implementing any strategies when deciding how to most effectively facilitate performance under pressure. Additionally, some of these strategies may be useful to implement during competition (e.g. self-talk is useful in sustaining confidence during competition; Jackman et al., [Citation2020](#)), whilst others are more suited to be implemented prior to competition (e.g. setting process-related goals prior to an event may increase task-focused attention; Hill et al., [Citation2009](#); Hill et al., [Citation2017](#); Hill & Shaw, [Citation2013](#)). As such, when implementing strategies, it is important to be aware of the context the athlete is competing in, as well as the individual factors already exhibited by the athlete.

## Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to review and meta-synthesise the existing qualitative literature on performance under pressure in sport. In doing so, we sought a greater understanding of the strategies and psychological factors central to performing under pressure. Meta-method analysis illustrated that wide a range of sports and athlete performance standards have been explored, and through several methodologies. Perspectives of key stakeholders have also been considered (i.e. coaches, sporting practitioners), however, the majority of research is athlete centred. Meta-theory analysis revealed that less than half of the studies included for review were approached through a specific philosophical lens (*n* = 13 studies adopted a specific philosophical underpinning), and the majority of studies did not utilise a guiding theoretical framework (*n* = 9 studies reported a theoretical framework). Additionally, the majority of studies were underpinned by traditional cognitive-behaviouralist assumptions, reflecting the historically dominant orientation of performance under pressure research. Specifically, researchers and practitioners informed by such 2nd-wave approaches primarily aim to support athletes to reduce or control the potential negative symptoms of pressure (i.e. competitive anxiety). Researchers and practitioners informed by other therapeutic approaches, however, may adopt a different perspective on athlete performance under pressure. For example, advocates of acceptance-based approaches (e.g. acceptance and commitment therapy; Hayes & Pierson, [Citation2005](#)) may recommend that athletes should accept that pressure is inherent in sport, where the focus shifts to performing with unpleasant experiences, rather than trying to control these unpleasant experiences (Svoboda et al., [Citation2024](#)). The overall meta-synthesis portrays the contextual and complex nature of athlete performance under pressure in sport, noting that varying individual factors and strategies are important in broadly facilitating athlete performance in these circumstances. It is also evident that the majority of literature included is very athlete centred, with little focus on other contextual factors (e.g. team dynamics; Cormack & Gillman, [Citation2021](#)). Whilst this may be attributed in part to our research questions and interpretation of findings, it does highlight the need for more qualitative research in this domain (i.e. investigating other contextual factors influencing performance). To prevent choking, it is important for athletes to minimise emotion-focused attention, debilitative interpretations of anxiety and situational demands, and unfamiliar situations. To promote clutch performance, athletes should seek to enhance task-focused attention, positively appraise situational demands (i.e. as within their capabilities), and maintain high self-efficacy.

The current review gathered data from key stakeholders – athletes, coaches, and sport psychologists – regarding athlete performance under pressure. That is, perspectives from athletes, coaches, and sporting practitioners were included to provide varying, yet important, insights into how athletes can perform well under pressure. A key takeaway from the meta-theory analysis in particular, is the need for theory that explains how athletes can achieve a clutch performance. A large majority of these results, and the broader field exploring performance under pressure, is centred on the prevention of choking. Whilst the prevention of choking is crucial for any athlete to be successful, clutch performances are also a highly regarded achievement. Given these results, there is a need for more research exploring clutch performance, which currently lacks in both investigation of the underpinning mechanisms of occurrence, and explanatory theory. Results regarding clutch performance focused primarily on definitional issues (e.g. Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#)), and in the broader literature, has often adopted theories of choking to explain how athletes may achieve a clutch performance (e.g. Gray & Cañal-Bruland, [Citation2015](#)). This adoption of choking theories, however, is problematic in clutch performance research, given the explanations’ reliance on the experience of anxiety (i.e. choking theories focus on performance breakdown in response to the experience of anxiety). As such, there remains a need for continued research exploring the occurrence and experience of clutch performance. This research can then act as a guiding framework for the development of future theory underpinning the occurrence of clutch performance in sport.

A notable finding of the present meta-study was the pivotal role that confidence and perceived control play in facilitating performance under pressure. Indeed, both confidence and perceived control have been previously associated with athletes performing well under pressure in quantitative research (e.g. Otten, [Citation2009](#)). Results of the present review suggested these constructs to reduce debilitative interpretations of anxiety (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2011](#)) and improve task-focused attention (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)), and thereby facilitate performance. There is ambiguity, however, when differentiating between these constructs. Specifically, whilst confidence and perceived control are often treated as distinct constructs, they are often defined and operationalised in a similar light. For example, in the broader literature, both confidence and perceived control have been utilised as the third dimension in models of anxiety (Cheng et al., [Citation2009](#); Martens, [Citation1990](#)). Additionally, athletes within the reviewed studies often expressed confidence in their ability to achieve the desired outcome, which was sometimes labelled as *confidence* (e.g. Swann et al., [Citation2017](#)), and other times as *perceived control* (e.g. Jordet et al., [Citation2006](#)). This confusion may be due to the definitional issues regarding these constructs, as Mesagno and Marchant ([Citation2013](#)) note, ‘there is no definitive understanding of perceived control and specifically whether it most closely relates to control over the situation, attention, behaviour, emotion, or all (or none) of these’ (p. 73). Further, whilst confidence may influence an athlete's perception of control (Otten, [Citation2009](#)), the precise relationship between these constructs remains unclear. That is, perceptions of control have also been previously considered to underpin athlete self-efficacy (e.g. Hanton & Connaughton, [Citation2002](#)). As such, whilst these two constructs may be crucial in facilitating performance under pressure, it is important for research to explore the relationship between them, and the definitional issues regarding perceived control, to further the development of interventions targeted at promoting athlete performance.

Extending on the importance of confidence and perceived control, it is crucial to consider these findings in light of other previous explanations of athlete performance. Specifically, self-efficacy has previously been linked to the occurrence of challenge states (in The Theory of Challenge and Threat States; Jones et al., [Citation2009](#)) and challenge appraisals (Swann et al., [Citation2022](#)), both of which can be likened to the facilitative appraisal of situational demands commonly reported in the reviewed studies (e.g. Mesagno & Marchant, [Citation2013](#)). These facilitative appraisals have previously been suggested to benefit athlete performance due to the additional mental resources they can allocate to the task (e.g. attention; Eysenck & Calvo, [Citation1992](#)). As a result, athletes appraising the situation as a challenge may experience less potentially negative consequences of anxiety (e.g. distraction), whilst benefitting from the motivational aspects (Jones et al., [Citation2009](#)). This benefit in athlete performance is supported in the findings of the present review, where increased task-focused attention was commonly reported to be important to athlete performance under pressure. As such, self-efficacy may benefit athlete performance under pressure through the facilitation of challenge appraisals, resulting in improved attention task-focused attention.

Another noteworthy finding is the significance of the attentional processes of athletes regarding performance under pressure. Similar to previous quantitative research on performance under pressure (e.g. Gucciardi & Dimmock, [Citation2008](#); Liao & Masters, [Citation2002](#)), and in support of current distraction models of choking, task-focused attention was considered as important for performing well under pressure (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2017](#)), whilst emotion-focused attention and distraction were linked to episodes of choking (e.g. Mesagno et al., [Citation2009](#)). Considering this, results from the present review note strategies that enhance task-focused attention to benefit athletes in performing under pressure (e.g. self-talk). Indeed, a review of choking interventions conducted by Gröpel and Mesagno ([Citation2019](#)) noted the benefit of task-focused attention in distraction-based choking interventions and emphasised the use of a pre-performance routine to prevent performance breakdown. These strategies may be especially useful to athletes that are prone to distraction when experiencing symptoms of anxiety. Additionally, findings also support self-focus models of choking, wherein process-oriented attention during skill execution can hinder performance by disrupting skill automaticity (see Mesagno & Beckmann, [Citation2017](#)). To address this, a pre-execution process-oriented focus (e.g. imagery, Hill et al., [Citation2011](#)), and adopting an external focus on the task during skill execution (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)), have shown benefits in enhancing performance. These strategies may be mostly suited to athletes exhibiting low self-efficacy, and high self-awareness (e.g. Mesagno et al., [Citation2008](#)). Of note, it may be the case that the abovementioned strategies and explanations of choking are suitable to apply under different circumstances (e.g. distraction theories may struggle, compared to self-focus models, in explaining performance breakdown due to high athlete self-awareness). Thus, it is important for athletes, coaches, and sporting practitioners to be aware of the attentional processes important to performing under pressure.

## Future directions and limitations

Based on the findings of the present review, the following suggestions for future research are offered. Firstly, whilst there is an abundance of research investigating choking in sport, there is a clear lack of research exploring the processes that underlie the occurrence of clutch performance. As such, the current review is limited by the availability of the qualitative research exploring the factors facilitating performance under pressure. Recent qualitative research (e.g. Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#); Swann et al., [Citation2017](#)) has contributed to the refinement of the definition of clutch performance, however, there is a need for research to explore the processes underlying clutch performance to further develop theory of, and interventions facilitating, performance under pressure in sport.

Another suggestion for future research to consider is the development of theory underpinning clutch performance. The current review highlights the need for a theoretical underpinning of clutch performance, given that several of the studies exploring clutch performance within this review were building a foundation from which a theory can be developed (e.g. definitional issues; Schweickle et al., [Citation2022](#), underlying psychological state; Swann et al., [Citation2017](#)). To address this issue, future research should continue exploring factors associated the occurrence of clutch performance (e.g. Hill et al., [Citation2010](#)), which in turn, will help facilitate the development of pressure training interventions targeting the promotion of clutch performance. In doing so, it is important to consider the appropriate research methods that are suited to developing further understanding of the processes important to facilitating clutch performance. That is, event-focused qualitative interviews (Jackman et al., [Citation2022](#)), mixed-method approaches, and case studies (Heale & Twycross, [Citation2018](#)), may all prove useful in developing theory to underpin the occurrence of clutch performance, given the current issues regarding quantitative exploration.

As with any research paper, this review is subject to several limitations. First, it is acknowledged that the decision to focus only on studies published in English may have impacted the findings of the present review. Further, we acknowledge that the present review was not pre-registered. With this in mind, we followed ENTREQ guidelines (Tong et al., [Citation2012](#)) to remain transparent and open to readers. Additionally, given the nature of qualitative analysis, it is possible that our interpretation of the findings will differ to that of other researchers. To address this, we sought the opinions of critical friends, where the multiple interpretations could be discussed. Finally, given that we primarily included articles based on their purpose (i.e. to explore perceptions of performance under pressure), we may have unintentionally excluded some articles with potentially relevant data. Whilst this may have been avoided by including articles based on the relevance of their findings, this comes with a number of additional challenges (e.g. the cut-off point for inclusion can be difficult to specify along with an extensive full text screening process).

In conclusion, this synthesis sheds light on crucial individual factors and effective strategies for enhancing performance under pressure in sports. High self-efficacy, positive appraisal of demands, mental toughness, and task-focused resource allocation are pivotal for athletes. To benefit these individual factors, athletes can adopt strategies such as pre-performance routines, imagery techniques, self-talk, goal setting, cognitive restructuring, and simulated pressure training. Additionally, this review highlights the inconsistencies in the terminology used to describe positive performances under pressure (e.g. clutch performance, choking resistance), and in capturing the pressure experience (i.e. stress, anxiety).
